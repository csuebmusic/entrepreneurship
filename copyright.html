Copyright cheat sheet

US copyright as it bears on a music practice, condensed from NYFA, chapters 3 and 4, and Andrews, chapter 5. Both books describe themselves as overviews; for your own situation, ask an attorney.

what's protected

Copyright subsists in an original work of authorship once it's fixed in a tangible medium, published or not, with or without registration. Originality requires only "a modicum of creativity." Fixation means notated, recorded, or otherwise set down in a form that can be perceived or reproduced; an idea shared in conversation has no protection, even if someone else then uses it.

Copyright protects expression, never the idea, fact, procedure, system, method, concept, or principle behind it. Star-crossed lovers from warring families aren't protectable; the book, score, and choreography of West Side Story are. Words, short phrases, slogans, symbols, and other identifiers usually fall outside copyright and may fall under trademark; systems, methods, and devices may fall under patent.

The eight statutory categories include musical works (with any accompanying words), dramatic works (with any accompanying music), choreographic works, audiovisual works, and sound recordings. A recording of a song carries two copyrights: the musical work and the sound recording, which can have different owners.

NYFA, ch. 3, Copyright, Elements of Copyrightability

the bundle of rights

The owner holds the exclusive right to do, and to authorize others to do, each of the following. Each right can be sold, licensed, or assigned separately, and a license can be limited by use, term, and territory.

rightwhat it covers
ReproductionCopies and phonorecords. A phonorecord is the object a sound recording is embodied in: cassette, CD, vinyl, and other formats.
Derivative worksArrangements, adaptations, translations, remixes. The derivative author owns only the new material, and only if it's sufficiently original; minor additions or rearrangements don't qualify.
DistributionSale, rental, lease, or lending of copies to the public.
Public performanceLiterary, musical, dramatic, and choreographic works, pantomimes, and audiovisual works.
Public displayLiterary, musical, dramatic, and choreographic works, pantomimes, and pictorial, graphic, and sculptural works, including the individual images of an audiovisual work.
Digital audio transmissionSound recordings only.
The copy and the copyright are separate property. Buying or streaming a recording gives you that copy for private use, and the rights stay with the songwriters, publishers, producers, and label. Selling a manuscript score or a painting conveys the object, and the copyright stays with the author unless the sales agreement transfers it.

NYFA, ch. 3, What Kind of Protections Does Copyright Give to an Artist?

who owns it

situationowner
DefaultThe author: the person who fixed the expression.
Employee, within the scope of employmentThe employer, automatically, as a work made for hire. Employment turns on factors such as salary, tax withholding, and control over the work and the worker (location, tools, schedule, assignment of other tasks), and whether the work fits the hiring party's regular business.
Independent contractor, commissionedThe author, unless both conditions hold: the work falls in a statutory category (contribution to a collective work, part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, translation, supplementary work, compilation, instructional text, test, answer material, atlas) and both parties signed a written agreement designating it a work made for hire. Then the commissioner is the author.
Commissioner who only paysNot a coauthor. Contributing money, ideas, or logistics doesn't create joint authorship; the artist keeps the copyright, subject to whatever use rights were granted.
Joint workCollaborators who set out to make the work together, intending a shared copyright, own undivided interests in the whole, regardless of the size of each contribution, unless a written agreement sets the shares and scope.
AssignmentWhoever the rights were assigned to, to the extent of the grant.

Ensembles and dance and theater companies are often made up of independent contractors with informal relationships, which is where ownership disputes start. NYFA's remedy is an agreement written before the work exists.

NYFA, ch. 3, Works Made for Hire, Joint Works; ch. 4, Statute of Frauds

using others' work

routeconditions
Public domainThe term has expired, or the work never qualified; US federal government works are public domain. Translations, arrangements, and editions of public-domain works can carry their own copyright, so clear the version you're using.
TermWorks created on or after January 1, 1978: life of the author plus 70 years, counted from the death of the last surviving author. Works made for hire and anonymous works: 95 years from publication or 120 from creation, whichever is shorter. Earlier works depend on the law in force when they were made and its later amendments.
LicenseNegotiated with the owner, usually for payment, sometimes gratis. An owner can refuse or price a license out of reach; then the work can't be used, subject to the exceptions below.
Compulsory (mechanical) license, §115Nondramatic musical works only, for making and distributing phonorecords to the public for private use. Pay the statutory royalty and serve notice on the owner within thirty days of making and before distributing; no negotiation needed. The arrangement may adapt the work to your style and may not change its basic melody or fundamental character, and it isn't protected as a derivative work without the owner's consent. It doesn't license duplicating someone else's sound recording unless that recording was lawfully fixed and its owner authorized the duplication. Licensing agents issue mechanical licenses on behalf of the publishers and songwriters they represent, and collect and distribute the royalties.
Fair use, §107A defense to infringement, not a license. See below.

NYFA, ch. 3, Term of Copyright, Using Others' Works, Compulsory Licenses

fair use

Courts weigh four statutory factors case by case, so outcomes are hard to predict and there's no safe quantity.

factorhow courts read it
Purpose and character of the useFavors noncommercial, nonprofit educational, critical, commentary, and parody uses. Transformative use, analyzed under this factor since Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music (1994), asks whether the new work uses the source in new or unexpected ways. Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023) narrowed it: new expression, meaning, or message isn't enough on its own, and a commercial use that serves substantially the same purpose as the original weighs against fair use. The Court held that licensing Warhol's silkscreen of Goldsmith's Prince photograph to a magazine wasn't favored by this factor.
Nature of the copyrighted workFactual works get thinner protection; using a highly imaginative work weighs against fair use.
Amount and substantialityMeasured against the original, not the new work, and qualitatively as well as quantitatively: a short excerpt can weigh heavily if it takes the "heart of the work."
Effect on the marketWeighs against fair use when the new work is a market substitute, takes sales from the original, or lets the user avoid a license and royalties.

Andrews notes that putting someone else's photographs or music on your site without permission can make you liable for infringement unless you can establish fair use.

NYFA, ch. 3, Fair Use; Andrews, ch. 5, pp. 154–155; US Copyright Office, case summary of Warhol v. Goldsmith

registration and infringement

  • Registration with the US Copyright Office isn't required for the copyright to exist. It is a precondition to suing for infringement, it creates a public record and a dated timeline of creation, and it makes the owner eligible for statutory damages (up to $30,000 per infringement, or $150,000 if willful, per NYFA) and attorneys' fees, often far more than actual damages. Filing is electronic and inexpensive, at copyright.gov.
  • Notice (©, the owner's name, "All Rights Reserved") isn't required and puts the world on notice.
  • Infringement requires access and copying with substantial similarity. Without proof of access there's no infringement, even if two works are identical.

NYFA, ch. 3, Copyright, Registration, Copyright Infringement

the grant of rights in a contract

The grant-of-rights clause defines which rights pass in exchange for the consideration. For any commission, recorded engagement, or collaboration, it should settle:

  • which of the six rights are granted and which the artist reserves;
  • whether the physical work (score, files, master) passes, and whether the copyright passes with it or is retained;
  • term, territory, and exclusivity;
  • whether the relationship is employment or a contractor engagement, and whether the agreement contains work-made-for-hire language: either can move ownership to the hiring party;
  • who owns, and who may use and post, recordings, video, and photographs of the work.

Put it in writing. Spoken agreements can bind, and emails, texts, and handwritten notes count as writings, though they rarely settle these terms. The Statute of Frauds requires a writing for, among others, any agreement that can't be performed within a year and any work-made-for-hire relationship.

NYFA, ch. 4, Grant of Rights, Statute of Frauds

adjacent protections

protectionfor an artist
Moral rightsUS law recognizes attribution and integrity only through the Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990, limited to paintings, drawings, prints, sculptures, and exhibition photographs in single copies or signed, numbered editions of 200 or fewer, with numerous exceptions. Musical works and recordings aren't covered. Works sold, made, or commissioned abroad may carry moral rights under other countries' laws.
Trademark and service markProtects a name, logo, or other source identifier: an ensemble name, a series name. Rights arise from use in commerce; USPTO registration adds a presumption of ownership and exclusivity for the listed goods or services. Refusals turn mostly on likelihood of confusion (a similar mark in a similar market); descriptive terms, geographic terms, surnames, and ornamental marks are also refused. Identical marks can coexist in unrelated markets. Registration renews indefinitely while the mark stays in use. Search the USPTO database before investing in a name.
Trade secretEconomically valuable know-how that copyright, trademark, and patent don't reach, such as a technique: NYFA's examples are a painter's way of mixing paint and a singer's way of breathing. The options are to never disclose it, or to bring others in under well-drafted confidentiality agreements.
Creative CommonsLicenses that let an owner share work on stated terms of attribution, modification, and commercial use.

NYFA, ch. 3, Special Rights for Visual Artists, Trademark and Service Mark, Trade Secrets; Andrews, ch. 5, pp. 155–157
