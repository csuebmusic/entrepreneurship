Creative Identity, Voice & Values

Movement I: orientation, identity, and the power lens

Last week you named who you are as an artist. This week goes underneath that, to the two things that make an identity work: your voice, the part of the work that is unmistakably yours, and your values, the internal compass that decides what you take on and what you turn down.

reading · McCurdy 2, Rabideau 3, Rabideau 4 reflection due · Mon Aug 31, before class component due · Fri Sep 4, Canvas

this week

The readings agree on one thing: voice and values are discovered, not declared. McCurdy treats the artist as a storyteller and sends you back to your own roots to find the story only you can tell. Rabideau, in the strengths chapter, reframes you as more than your instrument, and his sharpest figure is a flutist who stopped chasing the "right" job and built an internal compass instead, a short set of questions she checks her work against. In the creativity chapter he argues that your voice enters exactly where you stop being predictable, and he asks you to define creativity, and your artistic life, in your own words.

Read for the gap between stated values and governing values. It is easy to list the values you admire. It is harder, and more useful, to name the ones that have actually decided something for you, the ones you could hand to a future version of yourself standing in front of a real choice. That gap is the whole assignment this week, so notice where the readings push you toward the honest version rather than the flattering one.

the reading

Three chapters, one from McCurdy and two from Rabideau, circling the same question from voice, from strengths, and from creativity. Read them in any order.

McCurdy, ch. 2, "What's the Story?"

The voice chapter. McCurdy treats artists as storytellers and works outward from self-knowledge: who you are, where you come from, and what you are trying to say. Practical and exercise-driven.

look for · the opening questions (what is your story, your raison d'être) and the "artistic DNA" exercise that sends you into your own roots, plus his use of flow.

Rabideau, ch. 3, "Assessing Your Strengths" (pp. 31–42)

You are more than your instrument. Rabideau names the transferable strengths a creative career runs on, which he calls superpowers, and ties them directly to your values and beliefs.

look for · the flutist Sarah Robinson and her "internal compass," a short set of questions she uses to define success on her own terms, and Rosenfeld's six superpowers.

Rabideau, ch. 4, "Thinking about Creativity" (pp. 43–56)

Creativity as the place your unique voice enters, and a process you can actually work. He asks you to define creativity in your own words, which doubles as a way to articulate your artistic life.

look for · the five-stage creative process, the "define it in your own words" exercise, and Mark Clague's take on measured risk.

reflection due before class · Mon Aug 31

Thinking on paper, not a polished essay. It does not go in your portfolio, so use it to get honest before we talk.

In one to two pages: name two or three values that actually govern your creative decisions, not the ones you would put on a website, and give a real example of each. For each value, point to a specific time it shaped a choice you made, or a choice you turned down.

portfolio component due Fri Sep 4 · Canvas

Values Statement

A Values Statement names the values that guide your creative choices, written so they could guide an actual decision. It builds directly on last week's Creative Identity Statement: the identity says who you are, the values say how you choose. Later components lean on it, your mission and vision next week, and further out your positioning and your pricing, so keep it honest and specific rather than aspirational.

Questions to find it, answering the ones that matter for you:

  • What do you consistently protect in your work, even when it costs you something?
  • What kind of work do you say no to, and why?
  • Whose interests do you hold in view when you decide: your audience, your collaborators, your community?
  • Where do these values come from: your roots, your training, your experience?
  • If a future you faced a hard choice, which of these would actually decide it?

Write a Values Statement of roughly half a page to a page, naming two or three values. For each, say what it means in your practice and how it would guide a real decision, not just that you hold it. Aim for values that do work, not values that sound good. Submit it on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday.

A strong Values Statement is specific, honest about trade-offs, and usable: you could hand it to yourself before a hard choice and it would help. You build on it next week, when Mission and Vision put the identity and values you have now named into a direction.
