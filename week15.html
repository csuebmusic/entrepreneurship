Impact, Leadership & Futures

Module VI: impact, integration, futures

The last week looks up from the work to the difference it makes, and pulls the whole portfolio into one document: the societal impact you want your practice to have, and the through-line that runs across everything you have built.

reading · Rabideau 2, Andrews 7, McCurdy 8, Kolb 11 reflection due · Mon Nov 30, before class component due · Fri Dec 4, Canvas

this week

The course opened on who you are and how power and identity shape access. It closes by asking what difference your work makes. Rabideau frames the artist's curiosity as directed toward community impact and toward a why, the meaning under the work. Andrews sets that in a larger frame: the arts have a public value that societies fund and shape through cultural policy, and access, who gets to make and reach culture, has been the recurring question for centuries. Together they ask what societal impact you want your practice to have, and for whom.

Impact takes leadership and a sense of where you are going. McCurdy's argument is that leadership is learnable, built from your own strengths and grounded in communicating a vision clearly rather than only leading by example. Kolb turns to the practical futures: how a practice grows, through new channels, scale, or a change of form. Read the two for the same question in different registers, what you would lead your practice toward, and how it might grow to get there.

Across the term you have built sixteen components, and the last one is different. Read for the thread that connects your identity to your audience to your business model to your outreach.

the reading

Read them in any order.

Rabideau, ch. 2, "Exploring Curiosity: Finding Opportunities to Make a Community Impact" (pp. 17–30)

Curiosity as directed toward community impact, and the search for your why, the meaning under the work.

look for · the "find your why" move, and the artists who use their work to make others see something new.

Andrews, ch. 7, "Cultural Policy and the Arts Entrepreneur" (pp. 208–239)

The public frame: how societies fund and shape the arts through cultural policy, and how access has been the recurring question.

look for · the theme of access, or democratizing culture, and the idea that the arts have a public value.

McCurdy, ch. 8, "Arts Leadership"

Leadership in the arts: learnable, built from your strengths, and grounded in communicating a vision rather than only leading by example.

look for · the argument that leadership can be learned, and the emphasis on communicating your goals.

Kolb, ch. 11, "Expanding the business" (pp. 249–271)

The practical futures: how a practice grows, through new distribution, scale, or a change of form.

look for · the growth options (consignment, retail, a nonprofit form), and the trade-offs each involves.

Portfolio Overview Statement

A Portfolio Overview Statement is a short integrative introduction to your portfolio. It names the through-line that runs across your components. It is the first thing a reader meets, and it is the introduction to your final portfolio.

Questions to build it from:

  • What is the through-line across your components, the thread that connects your identity, audience, business model, and outreach?
  • Where do the components already agree, and where do they need to be brought into line?
  • What should a reader understand before they read the rest?
  • How would you introduce the whole, in a few short paragraphs?
  • What is the societal impact your practice is working toward, drawn from this week's reflection?
