Pricing & Money

Module III: business model and money

Pricing is where value meets money, and there is no single right number. This week you set a price and, harder, defend it.

reading · NYFA 13, Kolb 6, Landry 3, McCurdy 4 reflection due · Mon Oct 19, before class component due · Fri Oct 23, Canvas

this week

There is no single right price, but there are three honest ways to reach one. Cost-based pricing starts from what the work costs you to make, your fixed and variable costs, plus a margin. Market-based pricing starts from what comparable work charges and where you sit relative to it. Value-based pricing starts from what the work is worth to the buyer, the benefit they receive, which Kolb is careful to note does not mean cheap: value is worth measured against benefit.

Kolb and McCurdy agree on the most common error, which is underpricing. Creatives undervalue their own work, price low to make the sale, then have to raise prices later or come to resent the buyer. McCurdy is blunt about it, no more playing for punch and cookies, and adds a nuance worth keeping: value your work, and in the earliest stage let development, not money, set the pace. Landry supplies the ground under all of it, the budget, since you cannot price on cost without knowing your fixed and variable costs.

A price is also a gate. It decides not only whether you get paid but who can afford your work, which connects pricing back to the access questions from week 5. Established practices, a sliding scale (a range of prices set by what a buyer can pay), pay-what-you-can, or a community or student rate, let you hold a price and open a door at the same time. Read for the number you can defend, and for whom your pricing includes and excludes.

the reading

Four readings on the same problem, from methods, from the numbers under them, and from the mindset. Landry is read for its budgeting section; the rest are full chapters.

NYFA, ch. 13, "Pricing Your Art" (pp. 206–215)

Pricing across disciplines: cost as a baseline, value as a function of the market and your position, and discipline-specific norms.

look for · the caution against lowering an established price, and the advice to compare with artists at a similar career stage.

Kolb, ch. 6, "Mastering the numbers" (pp. 128–150)

The pricing approaches (cost, competition, prestige), the break-even formula, and the argument that value does not mean cheap. Also why underpricing is the common mistake.

look for · the three pricing approaches, and the point that a low price can signal low quality.

Landry, ch. 3, "Create with Constraints" (pp. 53–74)

Read the budgeting section: fixed versus variable costs, and the basic bookkeeping that tells you what your work actually costs to make.

look for · the fixed-versus-variable distinction, the number a cost-based price starts from.

McCurdy, ch. 4, "It's Not about the Money—Yet!"

The money mindset: value your work rather than give it away, and the nuance that in the earliest stage development matters more than money.

look for · the "punch and cookies" argument against underpricing, and when the authors say pay is warranted.

Pricing Rationale

A Pricing Rationale states your price for at least one offering and the reasoning behind it: which approach you used, cost, market, value, or a mix, and why. It also names how you thought about access, since a price decides who can afford your work.

Questions to build it from:

  • What are you pricing, and what does it cost you to make (fixed and variable)?
  • What do comparable offerings charge, and where do you sit relative to them?
  • What is the offering worth to the buyer, the benefit they receive?
  • What price will you set, and which approach or mix does it rest on?
  • How does your pricing handle access: whom does it include, and would a sliding scale, pay-what-you-can, or community rate fit?

Write a Pricing Rationale for at least one offering: the price you set, and the reasoning behind it, whether cost, market, value, or a mix. Include how you thought about access, such as a sliding scale or community rate. Submit it on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday.
