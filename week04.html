this week

An arts ecosystem is the whole field around a career: the collaborators, presenters, funders, venues, and audiences that a path runs through. McCurdy's self-management chapter is the most concrete about it. Without a manager, you are your own agent, and the field resolves into specific players: presenters who decide what gets booked, venues like college centers and museums, patrons, and the network you build. Andrews widens the view to the roles inside an organization, the trends reshaping the field, and the plain tools of a career, the résumé, the cover letter, the work samples, the references. Two of those tools are your work this week, so read his Career Development section closely.

Both McCurdy's "creative space" chapter and Andrews say the same thing about the paths themselves: the old full-time track has thinned, so careers now run interdisciplinary and portfolio-shaped, built as much through relationships as through credentials. Hold onto McCurdy's blunt version of it, that technical skill may get you hired once, but relationships get you hired again.

The map is not neutral, and that is the part to read for. Presenters and funders are gatekeepers, and some paths are open to some people and closed to others. Rabideau's epilogue says it outright, in Stanford Thompson's work against racial inequality in classical music and in artists who bring their work to audiences that have been denied access. As you map your own field, mark where the power sits.

The long arc: how a single project becomes a career, through leadership, community, and access. It names the access question directly.

Three components this week: the map of the field, and the two documents that field asks for when you approach it. The résumé and the cover letter both draw on the Creative Identity Statement and Values Statement you have already written.

Career Ecosystem Map

A Career Ecosystem Map is a picture of the field around one path you are considering: the people, organizations, venues, and revenue points that make it up, and where you fit among them. It can be an actual diagram or a structured, annotated list. Unlike the statements so far, this component looks outward, at the terrain rather than at you.

Questions to build it from:

  • What path are you mapping, and who are the people and organizations in it: collaborators, presenters, funders, audiences, employers?
  • Where are the gatekeepers, the ones who decide what gets booked, funded, or shown?
  • Where does the money come from, the revenue points along this path?
  • Where do you sit in this map now, and where do you want to move?
  • Where does power sit, and how does access to this path get distributed?

Map the ecosystem around your chosen path, as a diagram or an annotated list. Include the key people, organizations, venues, and revenue points; mark the gatekeepers and where power sits; and locate yourself in it. Submit on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday.

Résumé

An artist's résumé is the compact professional record of your work: education and training, positions held, performances, exhibitions, commissions, publications, teaching, awards, and the skills that go with them. In academic and grant contexts the same material often appears as a curriculum vitae, which runs longer and lists everything; the résumé is the version edited down for a particular reader. Andrews describes two common shapes, and either is defensible. A chronological résumé lists education and employment most recent first, which suits a record with steady positions in it. A functional résumé groups experience under skills and themes rather than dates, which suits a practice built from projects, commissions, and freelance work, and it lets coursework and student projects carry real weight.

Build the general version now, and customize it later for a specific opportunity.

  • Which shape fits the record you actually have: chronological, functional, or a hybrid?
  • What are your categories, and in what order does a reader in your field expect them?
  • Which entries carry the most weight, and which are filling space?
  • For each entry, does the reader learn the role, the organization, the place, and the date?
  • What skills, languages, software, and specialized training belong on it?

Write a résumé of one to two pages for your own practice, in whichever shape fits your record. Use consistent formatting, real dates, and specific entries. Submit it on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday, as a PDF so the layout holds.

Cover Letter

A cover letter is the one-page argument that goes with an application: why this opportunity, why you, and what the reader will find in the materials attached. The résumé lists what you have done. The letter says what it means for the thing being asked for, which is why a letter written for no particular opportunity reads as though it were written for no particular reader.

So write yours against a real one. Find a posting you could plausibly apply to now or within a year: a job, an adjunct or teaching position, a residency, a commission, a fellowship, an ensemble or festival call. Submit the posting with the letter.

  • What is the opportunity, and what does the posting actually ask for?
  • Where do your skills and interests meet what they are asking for, in specifics rather than adjectives?
  • Who are you addressing, and what do they already know about you?
  • What is the evidence for each claim you make, and where in your materials does it sit?
  • Does the letter sound like the artist described in your Creative Identity Statement?

Write a cover letter of up to one page for a real opportunity, and submit the posting alongside it. Open by naming the position and the organization, make the case for your qualifications against what the posting asks, point to the materials you would send with it, and close on the interview or next conversation. Submit both on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday.
