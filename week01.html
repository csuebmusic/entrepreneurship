Art, Culture & the Creative Career

Movement I: orientation, identity, and the power lens

We open with a question that sounds simple and is not: what does it mean to build a career as an artist? This week's four readings come at it from different angles, and give you the anchor your whole portfolio refers back to.

reading · Kolb 1, Andrews 1, Rabideau 1, McCurdy 1 before class · a one to two page reflection portfolio · Creative Identity Statement

this week

The four readings answer the opening question differently, but they agree on one move: they take "entrepreneurship" back from money-making. Here it means seeing an opportunity and building something real from the skills and resources you already have, nearer to invention than to commerce. Money still matters, but as a means, the thing that buys back time to make the work.

Read for the disagreements as much as the agreements. Rabideau treats the artist and the entrepreneur as the same animal: curious, collaborative, tenacious, at home in uncertainty. Kolb sets art inside a long history of markets and puts two questions in front of you that you will keep facing, whether to sell through an intermediary (the agent, gallery, or label that reaches an audience for a cut) or directly, and whether to build a nonprofit or a business. Andrews argues that an arts venture need not disrupt anything, that it usually starts from your values rather than from a product, and that a lifetime practice is a different thing from a venture built to be sold. McCurdy is the bluntest about the economy you are entering, and the clearest that the work begins with deciding who you are as an artist.

One thread runs under all four and through the whole course. Rabideau and McCurdy both insist that artistry belongs to everyone, in every community, and that a creative career is partly about doing good where it is needed. That claim, that your work has an effect beyond your own success, is where societal impact enters, and it sets up the harder questions about power and access in week 5. Hold onto it as you read.

the reading

Four short chapters, one from each anchor and rotation text, each opening the same question from its own vantage. Read them in any order; they talk to each other.

Kolb, ch. 1, "Art, culture and entrepreneurship" (pp. 8–30)

The business foundation. Kolb separates art's aesthetic value from its monetary value and lays out the three forms a venture can take (government, nonprofit, business). The most conventionally "business" of the four, and its vocabulary returns in later weeks.

look for · his claim that entrepreneurship is "just another means of creative expression," and the two questions he leaves you with: intermediary or direct, and nonprofit or business.

Andrews, ch. 1, "What Is Arts Entrepreneurship?" (pp. 1–34)

The academic map, and the longest read: from the textbook definitions (Drucker, Schumpeter, disruption) to lean-startup ideas like the minimum viable product, then to what makes an arts venture its own thing.

look for · the section on building a venture from your personal and professional values, and the contrast between open-ended arts ownership and the conventional exit strategy.

Rabideau, ch. 1, "Being a 21st-Century Musician" (pp. 1–16)

The music-native manifesto, and the book's spine: five mindsets (curiosity, creativity, collaboration, persistence, critical optimism) and three skill sets (storytelling, funding, moving ideas to action). Written for musicians, but it reads across disciplines.

look for · the portraits of working artists (Anderson & Roe, Lara Downes, Yo-Yo Ma) and the specific thing each did to make an opportunity rather than wait for one.

McCurdy, ch. 1, "A Model for Success"

A blunt account of the current arts economy and a model for working inside it. It begins where you will, with the question of who you are as an artist.

look for · the section "Defining Yourself as an Artist," the caution about "fear-based education" (defaulting to more school), and the three qualities he names for the twenty-first-century artist.

reflection due before class

Thinking on paper, not a polished essay. It gets your ideas moving before Monday so you arrive with something to workshop, and it does not go in your portfolio.

In one to two pages, do three things:

  • Say what "entrepreneurship" means for an artist in these readings, in your own words rather than a dictionary's.
  • Locate yourself in that picture, as honestly as you can right now, at whatever stage you are actually at.
  • Name one assumption the readings challenged for you, and say whether you buy it.

Bring it ready to talk.

portfolio component goes in your portfolio

Creative Identity Statement

Your portfolio is the professional document you build across the term, one component a week, and this first one anchors it. A Creative Identity Statement is a short, first-person statement of who you are as an artist and the work you do. Every later component refers back to the artist described here, so aim for honest over perfect. Write it so a stranger who read nothing else of yours could still say what you make and what you are after.

Questions to find it, answering the ones that matter for you rather than all of them:

  • Who are you as an artist, and what do you make?
  • What draws you to this work, and what are you trying to do or say through it?
  • What do you value in the work, and what would you not compromise?
  • Where are you right now: a student, an emerging artist, mid-career, changing direction?
  • What makes your perspective yours, the thing someone would miss if you were not doing it?

Write roughly half a page to a page, in the first person and in plain prose. Reach for the specific over the general: not "meaningful work that connects people," but the actual work and the actual concerns. Keep it readable outside your field, and true to where you are now.

A strong statement is concrete, honest about your stage, and unmistakably yours. You build on it next week with your Values Statement, again in week 3 with Mission and Vision, and revise it into the opening of your finished portfolio.
