Understanding Audiences

Module II: audience, positioning, product

Module II turns from who you are to who your work is for. This week that means your audience: the specific people who actually value your work, rather than everyone who might conceivably encounter it.

reading · Kolb 5, plus sections of NYFA 11, Landry 2, Andrews 3 reflection due · Mon Sep 21, before class component due · Fri Sep 25, Canvas

this week

All four readings reject the same instinct: that your audience is everyone. Kolb calls the people who actually value your work a segment, a group who share what they want from it, and argues you target a segment rather than broadcast to all. His warning is worth holding: you may start by selling to friends, but that does not make them your market.

Landry and NYFA get concrete about drawing that audience. They separate demographic facts (where people live, their age, their income) from psychographic ones (their values, tastes, and what they care about), and treat the psychographic as the more telling of the two. Both build the picture into a composite sketch of a real audience member, asking who they are, where they are, and how they found you. Andrews reframes the whole exercise as an exchange: people give you their attention, time, or money in return for something they value, which in the arts is often a feeling more than an object. Understanding your audience means understanding what they get from that trade.

Read for the gap between the audience you imagine and the one you have. Most of us assume we know who shows up, and most of us are partly wrong. Notice what you actually know about your audience and what you are guessing. A plan aimed at a guessed audience aims at no one.

the reading

One full chapter and three sections. Read only the sections named.

Kolb, ch. 5, "Targeting the right customer" (pp. 104–127)

The segmentation argument: audiences come in segments, and you target one rather than promote to all. Covers market depth versus breadth and the shift from demographic to psychographic grouping.

look for · why "everyone" is not a target, and the difference between fishing the same pond and casting wider.

NYFA, ch. 11, "Marketing Principles and Strategies" section: Understanding Your Audience

The who, what, where, when, and why of the person who actually shows up, built into a composite sketch, plus how they found you.

look for · the composite-sketch method, and the point that artists often have direct access to their audience.

Landry, ch. 2, "Build and Test," section "Defining Your Audiences" (pp. 29–37)

A practical toolkit: demographic and psychographic checklists for drawing a specific buyer, and a warning against "everyone is my customer."

look for · the psychographic list (values, tastes, what they care about), and the reminder that the value people seek may be a feeling.

Andrews, ch. 3, "Marketing," sections "The Exchange Model" and "Market Research and Competitive Analysis" (pp. 70–72)

Marketing as a trade of value, and research as how you learn what your audience wants and how they choose.

look for · the exchange model, and what market research asks about how people decide and what they treat as barriers.

portfolio component due Fri Sep 25 · Canvas

Audience Profile

An Audience Profile is a specific picture of the people who value your work: who they are, what they want from it, where they are, and how they find work like yours. Draw it as a composite of a real audience member rather than a category, and keep it to your core audience rather than everyone. This is the first piece of your marketing plan.

Questions to build it from:

  • Who is your core audience, in specifics: their demographic facts, and the more telling psychographic ones (values, tastes, what they care about)?
  • What do they actually value in work like yours, and what do they get from the exchange, which may be a feeling rather than an object?
  • Where are they, in place and online, and where do they already go for work like yours?
  • How do they find work like yours: who or what do they trust, and which channels do they use?
  • Which of this do you know, and which are you inferring and should test?

Write an Audience Profile of your core audience: who they are (demographic and psychographic), what they value, where they are, and how they find work like yours. Draw a specific, composite picture rather than "everyone," and flag what you are still inferring. Submit it on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday.
