Legal & Professional Issues

Module V: legal and financial sustainability

This week is about knowing enough law to protect your work and recognize the issue in front of you, and knowing when to bring in a professional. There is no portfolio component; the work is a worked case.

this week

The goal is legal awareness, not legal advice: enough to recognize an issue, protect your work, and know when you need an attorney. The readings cover four areas. Intellectual property is the first: copyright protects original work, and NYFA's distinction is that owning a copy of something is not the same as owning the rights to it. The same care that protects your work keeps you from infringing on someone else's. Contracts are the second, which NYFA calls road maps to relationships: they record who owes what to whom, and while a verbal agreement can bind, the durable lesson is to get it in writing.

The third area is how you organize the venture itself. NYFA and Andrews both walk the structures, from the sole proprietorship (simple to start, but your personal assets are fully exposed) through the limited liability company, the corporation, and the not-for-profit, and both frame the choice around two questions: liability and tax. The fourth is your rights as an artist and the risks worth insuring against. Both books are careful to say the same: these are overviews rather than advice for your situation, and the professional move is to know the concepts well enough to ask a lawyer or an accountant the right questions.

Read like a professional who will not become a lawyer but will have to work with one.

the reading

Four legal chapters. They are dense. Read for the concepts and the questions they raise rather than for rules to memorize.

NYFA, ch. 3, "Intellectual Property" (pp. 55–74)

Copyright, trademark, patent, and trade secret. The core is copyright: what it protects, and the difference between owning a copy and owning the rights.

look for · the point that buying a copy is not buying the rights, and the two-sided lesson of protecting your work and not infringing others'.

NYFA, ch. 4, "Contracts" (pp. 75–82)

What makes an agreement a contract, the common clauses, and which agreements the law requires in writing.

look for · the idea of a contract as a road map to a relationship, and the case for putting it in writing.

NYFA, ch. 5, "Business Entities" (pp. 83–103)

The structures a venture can take, sole proprietor through corporation, LLC, and not-for-profit, framed around liability and tax.

look for · the unlimited personal liability of a sole proprietor, and how the structures trade simplicity against protection.

Andrews, ch. 5, "Legal Issues" (pp. 145–177)

A wider legal overview: choosing a corporate form, artists' rights under the law, tax, insurance, and governance. It opens by stating it is not legal advice.

look for · the disclaimer and what it implies, and the section on artists' rights.

Take one ethics-and-legal case from the readings, a contract, a question of rights or intellectual property, or a scenario of professional conduct, and work it through. How would you handle it? What would you need to find out, and when would you bring in a professional? About two to three pages.
