## 6:05 week 4, the readings

The four readings assembled the field a career moves through, the paths across it, and where power sits in it.

- Andrews, chapter 8 (pp. 240–274). Organizational design first, then Career Development: the résumé, the biography, the artist statement, the cover letter written for one opening, references, and a portfolio of work samples. The chapter closes on Future Trends, where the field is heading: social-benefit organizations working with underserved populations, creative placemaking with developers and city planners, technology and science partnerships, crowdfunding and impact investing, co-creation with audiences, and entrepreneurial bricolage.

The alternative and interdisciplinary paths he points to include arts administration and music business. The players: presenters, who decide what gets booked; venues including college performing-arts centers, libraries, museums, and cultural centers; patrons and donors in decision-making positions; and your mailing list. Also anchor dates, block booking, the call to a presenter, and knowing your value before you make it.
- Rabideau, Epilogue (pp. 147–152). The arc from one project to a career, through leadership and community, and the access question stated directly: Dale Henderson playing Bach in the subway, Laurie Rubin on difference and disability, Stanford Thompson's Play On, Philly! on racial inequality in classical music.

Rabideau's epilogue names access as something artists provide or withhold. This week asks how access came to be distributed that way.

## 6:25 week 4, the three components

All three were due Friday, September 11. They're drafts, and every one of them gets revised into the final portfolio.

### Career Ecosystem Map

Check yours for these:

- One specific path, rather than your field in general.
- Real names. Actual presenters, venues, ensembles, labels, funders, and employers you could contact this year.
- Gatekeepers marked, each one paired with the decision it makes: what gets booked, what gets funded, what gets shown, who gets hired.
- Revenue points, including the ones that pay now and the ones that pay in three years.
- Your own location on the map, and the distance between where you are and where you're moving.

The fifth question on the page asked where power sits. Bring that mark to the first exercise.

### Résumé

Andrews gives two shapes and a hybrid. Chronological runs education and employment most recent first, with the institution, dates, degree, and major under education, and the title, employer, location, dates, and responsibilities under employment. Functional groups experience under skills and themes, and coursework and student projects count in it.

Check yours for these:

- The shape fits the record you have.
- Each entry gives the role, the organization, the place, and the date.
- Formatting is consistent down the page, and the file is a PDF.
- Skills, languages, software, and specialized training appear.
- Publications and conferences relevant to your area appear.

### Cover Letter

Andrews's sequence: address the recipient, state your qualifications precisely, name the parts of the posting your skills and interests meet, mention the materials you're sending, and close on the interview.

Check yours for these:

- The posting came with it.
- Specifics rather than adjectives against what the posting asks for.
- The claims have evidence, and the evidence sits in the materials you'd send.
- It sounds like the artist in your Creative Identity Statement.

Andrews's test is worth running before you revise: have someone read the letter aloud to you while you listen as the hiring manager.

## 7:05 Rabideau chapter 7

Laura Hlavacek Rabideau wrote this chapter. She's a human resources executive with a BM in flute performance and an MBA, and the lens is the individual and the team rather than the structure. Mark Rabideau frames it and tells you the discussion may feel uncomfortable.

Her definitions. Diversity is all the ways we differ: race, ethnicity, gender and gender identity, sexual orientation, age, religion, disability, socioeconomic status, education, and cognitive style. Inclusion puts that mix into action, in an environment where each person is respected and involved. She quotes Andrés Tapia: "Diversity is the mix, and inclusion is making the mix work."

Implicit bias is the chapter's central idea: subconscious assumptions about people unlike ourselves, acting on our decisions beneath our awareness. She separates bias in general, a preference for one thing over another, from unfair preference or prejudice. Her claim is that implicit associations can be gradually unlearned once we notice them, and her first step is introspection: notice whom you automatically seek out as a collaborator, and the patterns in who's around you.

Her case is the blind audition, which we examine after the break.

Three more sections and two guest voices:

- The inclusive team, modeled on a jazz trio, where each voice leads and then supports. Her test questions are about who talks, who's built on, and whether the group can hold a difficult conversation.
- Mary Pauline Sheridan-Rabideau on leading while discriminated against, built on a line of Beyoncé's about being the boss rather than bossy.
- Community engagement done with a community rather than for one: identify the need together, decide together, share responsibility.
- The access organizations she points to: the Ritz Chamber Players, Project STEP in Boston, and the Sphinx Organization.
- Mike Block and the Global Musician Workshop, where musicians from more than forty countries learn each other's music by ear.

Aaron Dworkin's essay runs pp. 80–84 and is the longest piece in the chapter. He describes himself by naming several identities at once, Black and white, Jewish and Irish Catholic, a violinist. He learned that Black composers existed while working on his degrees at Michigan, when a teacher asked whether he wanted to play their music and pulled the volumes off the shelf: William Grant Still, Coleridge-Taylor Perkinson, David Baker, Joseph Boulogne. He founded Sphinx as an undergraduate. He cites the League of American Orchestras on the coming decade's new audiences being largely audiences of color, and Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie on a single story being incomplete rather than untrue.

Discussion, in this order:

1. Dworkin's account is one person's education, twenty years of an organization, and a claim about what the field owes its audiences. Which of those three is evidence, and of what?
2. The chapter cites Deloitte Australia research that inclusive teams outperform their peers by 80 percent in team-based assessments. What would that number have to measure before it changed how you pick collaborators?
3. Sheridan-Rabideau's advice is addressed to the person facing discrimination. Whose behavior does it ask to change?
4. The chapter's remedy for bias is introspection. Name one decision in your own practice where introspection would be enough, and one where it wouldn't.

## 7:40 the evidence, Goldin and Rouse

The study behind Rabideau's example. Claudia Goldin and Cecilia Rouse collected audition records from eight major American orchestras across the decades when screens came in, and compared the same musicians auditioning with and without a screen.

What they report:

- Women were under 5 percent of players in the top five US orchestras in 1970 and about 25 percent when the paper was written.
- The screen raises the probability a woman advances out of certain preliminary rounds by about 50 percent, and raises the likelihood of a woman winning the final round by severalfold.
- The switch to blind auditions accounts for 30 to 55 percent of the rise in the share of women among new hires, and 25 to 46 percent of the rise in the share of women in the orchestras, since 1970.
- Their own caveats, in the abstract: several estimates carry large standard errors, and one effect runs persistently in the opposite direction.

The finding has been contested. In 2019 the statistician Andrew Gelman went through the paper and judged the data too noisy for strong conclusions, saying he couldn't reconstruct the 50 percent figure from what's printed. Goldin's response was that the figure comes from the musicians who auditioned both ways and applies only to certain rounds, as the paper states. A 2017 randomized study by behavioral economists in the Australian Public Service, "Going Blind to See More Clearly," asked more than 2,000 managers to shortlist from résumés, some with the applicant's sex removed. Removing it reduced the likelihood that women were shortlisted.

Three questions:

1. Rabideau presents the 50 percent as settled. What changes about the chapter if the number doesn't hold?
2. The screen removes one signal at one moment in a process. Name what it can't reach: who trained, who could afford the audition travel, who was told to apply.
3. An intervention can be worth keeping on grounds other than a measured effect. What are those grounds here, and who decides them?

## 8:00 the evidence, Panic!

Brook, O'Brien and Taylor, UK data, published by Create London in 2018. A survey of 2,487 creative workers with 237 follow-up interviews, set against national datasets: the Labour Force Survey, the ONS Longitudinal Study, the British Social Attitudes Survey, and Taking Part. The scope is nine industries, from advertising to music, performing and visual art.

Part 1, what workers believe. Asked what matters for getting ahead, 30 percent of respondents landed on talent and hard work while rejecting class and connections, 34 percent named both merit and social barriers, 21 percent emphasized barriers, and 16 percent neither. The respondents most committed to merit and most skeptical that class, gender, or ethnicity matter were the best paid, those earning over £50,000, regardless of where they started in life. The same survey asked who respondents know: they knew other creatives, and were unlikely to know factory workers, bus drivers, or postal workers.

Part 2, who's actually there. Share the figures on screen.

- Black and minority ethnic workers: 2.7 percent in museums, galleries and libraries; 4.2 percent in film, TV, video, radio and photography; 4.8 percent in music, performing and visual art.
- Working-class origins: 18.2 percent in music, performing and visual art; 12.6 percent in publishing; 12.4 percent in film, TV and radio. Around a third of the wider population has those origins.
- Pay: women across the creative industries earn an estimated £5,800 a year less than comparable men, and about £15,000 less in film and TV. In publishing the class-origin gap reaches £23,000, though the report ties much of that to education levels. The gender gap it does not.
- Over time: young cultural workers from upper-middle-class origins went from 15 percent in 1981 to 33 percent in 2011, and those from working-class origins from 22 percent to 13 percent. The chances of getting in, by social origin, held steady across those thirty years. Britain's class structure is what changed.
- London holds most of the jobs. 34.8 percent of its creative workforce comes from upper-middle-class origins, against 23 percent elsewhere in the UK, and 17 percent are BAME in a city whose working-age population is 39 percent BAME.

Two mechanisms from the later parts, which you weren't asked to read. Around 87 percent of respondents had worked for free, and the interviews split on class origin: for those with family support it was a choice and a favor to colleagues, for those without it was a barrier and, in their own words, exploitation. And hiring can run as cultural matching, where shared taste is what a candidate is read for. Creative workers' attendance and tastes sit far from the rest of the population's, which makes that filter a narrow one.

Discussion:

1. The high earners in Part 1 are the people who set hiring practice. What does that pattern predict about how change happens in this sector?
2. The class-origin chances held steady from 1981 to 2011 while the composition changed sharply. Which of the two would you want a policy to move, and what would it have to do?
3. This is UK data. Which findings would you expect to travel to the US field you work in, and which wouldn't? Name what you'd need to check.
4. Goldin and Rouse study one decision point. Panic! studies a whole labor market. What can each see that the other can't?

## 8:40 the Positionality and Access Statement

One to two pages, due Friday, September 18, by 11:59 pm on Canvas. This one stays in the portfolio, and weeks 6 through 15 refer back to it.

Positionality is your location in the field: the background, training, resources, and social identity that shape the access you have and the barriers you meet. The statement is your account of that location, what it opens, what it closes, and what both mean for the framework you're building.

- You decide what goes in it. Nothing in the brief requires disclosure of anything you'd rather keep out of a document you submit.
- Access counts as much as barriers. A conservatory network, a city, an instrument you already own, a language, family who can absorb a lean year, a teacher who made a call for you.
- Name mechanisms rather than feelings about them. Tonight's readings give you the vocabulary: gatekeeping, networks, unpaid work, cultural matching, the cost of the credential.
- End on the framework. What you plan around and what you push against, in terms specific enough to change a decision in your Career Ecosystem Map.
- There's no conclusion you're expected to reach.

Read it against your Creative Identity Statement and your Values Statement before you submit. If the position you describe here contradicts the artist described there, that's a revision item for the portfolio rather than a problem with either one.
