## the question tonight

Three readings, one question: who gets to build a career in the arts, and what decides it.

Rabideau puts the answer inside the individual. We act on assumptions we never examine, and examining them changes who we hire, program, and play with. Goldin and Rouse test a procedural fix at one decision point, the screen at an orchestra audition, and their result has been argued over since. Panic! measures a whole sector and finds the barriers sitting before any single decision: who you already know, who can afford to work unpaid, whose taste reads as taste.

Two diagnoses, two remedies. Tonight we take both seriously and ask which one reaches the decisions on your own map.

## 6:05 week 4, the readings

Together the four say one thing: a career doesn't happen in a field, it happens through a short list of people who make decisions about you.

McCurdy's chapter 6 names them. Presenters decide what gets booked. Most of them see you at an arts conference showcase, thirty minutes long, on day three or four when they're saturated, and an agent who doesn't represent you will sometimes sell you a slot in the room they've rented. Beyond concert halls there are college performing-arts centers, libraries, museums, and cultural centers, usually programming around a theme or a series, and readier to book you if you bring teaching with you. Before you name a fee, budget the whole presentation: an all-inclusive contract pays fees, travel, hotel, transport, and food in one check, and a venue that can't meet your number can sometimes cover rooms and meals instead. Once one date is confirmed, look at every venue within 150 miles of it. His case is a concert at the University of Kansas: fly into Kansas City once and add two or three performances around it.

Andrews, chapter 8, supplies the documents that field asks for: résumé, biography or artist statement, a cover letter written for one opening, references, work samples (pp. 253–261). He gives two résumé shapes, pp. 254–255. Chronological lists education and employment most recent first. Functional groups the work under themes, which lets coursework and projects count. His Future Trends section describes where the money and the jobs are moving: social-benefit organizations, creative placemaking with city planners and developers, science and technology partnerships, crowdfunding, co-creation with audiences.

McCurdy's chapter 3 is the labor market underneath all that. More trained graduates than full-time positions, and the paths that opened next to the old one run through arts administration, music business, and interdisciplinary programs. Jeff Coffin's line: technical skill can get you hired once, and relationships and professionalism get you hired again.

Rabideau's epilogue, pp. 147–152, adds the part tonight starts from. Access is something artists and institutions hand out or withhold: Dale Henderson playing Bach in the subway, Laurie Rubin on difference and disability, Stanford Thompson's Play On, Philly! against racial inequality in classical music. The question this week asks is how access came to be distributed that way in the first place.

## 7:05 Rabideau chapter 7

Laura Hlavacek Rabideau wrote the body of the chapter. She's a human resources executive with a BM in flute performance and an MBA, and she works at the level of the individual and the team. The chapter opens by telling you the discussion may feel uncomfortable and asking you to stay with it.

Her argument in short. Diversity is all the ways people differ, race, ethnicity, gender and gender identity, sexual orientation, age, religion, disability, class, education, and how they think. Inclusion is what makes that mix function, and she quotes Andrés Tapia: "Diversity is the mix, and inclusion is making the mix work" (p. 74). What keeps the mix from working is implicit bias, the assumptions about people unlike us that we act on without noticing. Preferring Bach to Stravinsky is a harmless bias; the same machinery decides who you call for a gig. Her claim is that these associations can be unlearned once you see them, and her method is introspection: whom do you automatically seek out as a collaborator, and what do the people around you have in common (p. 76). Read those two questions aloud.

Her evidence is the blind audition (pp. 75–76). The top five US orchestras were 95 percent men in the 1970s. Women reached about 25 percent by 1997. Orchestras had started putting a screen between the player and the jury, and a screen in the preliminary round alone raised a woman's chance of reaching the final by half. We take that apart after the break.

The rest of the chapter applies the idea: a jazz trio as the model of a team where each voice leads and then supports (pp. 76–77), her daughter Mary Pauline on leading while facing discrimination (p. 77), community work done with a community rather than for one (pp. 77–78), and three organizations built to open the field to musicians of color, the Ritz Chamber Players, Project STEP, and Sphinx (p. 78).

Aaron Dworkin's essay, pp. 80–84, is the longest piece in the chapter and the one to spend time on. Dworkin founded Sphinx. He was born to an unwed couple in 1970, adopted at two weeks by a white Jewish couple in New York, moved at ten to a Pennsylvania town where his was nearly the only Black family at school, and trained at Interlochen, Penn State, and Michigan. He reached graduate study before learning that Black composers existed, when a teacher asked whether he'd like to play their music and started pulling volumes off the shelf: William Grant Still, Coleridge-Taylor Perkinson, David Baker, Joseph Boulogne. He asks why nobody had told him about George Bridgetower, who premiered the Kreutzer Sonata with Beethoven in 1803. He founded Sphinx as an undergraduate, and it's now more than twenty years old. He quotes the League of American Orchestras on the next decade's new audiences being largely audiences of color, and Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie on a single story being incomplete rather than untrue.

Read aloud the paragraph on p. 81 where the teacher pulls the volumes off the shelf. Then ask how many of those four composers the room can name a piece by.

Discussion:

1. Dworkin's essay holds three different things: one person's education, twenty years of an organization, and a claim about what the field owes its audiences. Which of them is evidence, and of what? His own story establishes that a well-trained violinist reached graduate school without meeting a Black composer. It doesn't establish how common that was. Sphinx is the harder evidence, and what it can answer is whether an access organization changes who enters the field.
2. Her remedy for bias is introspection. Name one decision in your practice where that would be enough, and one where it wouldn't. Push on the second: anything decided by a panel, a budget, or a deadline.
3. The chapter cites Deloitte Australia research that inclusive teams outperform their peers by 80 percent in team-based assessments, and gives no citation for it (p. 74). What would that number have to measure before it changed how you choose collaborators?

## 7:40 Goldin and Rouse

What they had is close to an experiment. Orchestras adopted screens at different times and for different rounds, so Claudia Goldin and Cecilia Rouse could take the audition records of eight major American orchestras and compare the same musician judged with her sex visible and with it hidden.

What they report:

- Women were under 5 percent of players in the top five US orchestras in 1970, about 25 percent when the paper was written.
- The screen raises a woman's probability of advancing out of certain preliminary rounds by about 50 percent, and raises her chance of winning a final round by severalfold.
- Blind auditions account for 30 to 55 percent of the rise in the share of women among new hires since 1970.
- Their own caveats: several estimates have large standard errors, and one effect is persistently in the opposite direction.

This is the number Rabideau repeats, and it has been argued over. In 2019 the statistician Andrew Gelman worked through the paper, judged the data too noisy for strong conclusions, and said he could not reconstruct the 50 percent from what's printed. Goldin's reply was that the figure comes from the musicians who auditioned both ways and covers only certain rounds. A 2017 randomized study in the Australian Public Service, "Going Blind to See More Clearly," had more than 2,000 managers shortlist candidates from résumés with and without the applicant's sex shown, and removing sex made women less likely to be shortlisted.

Discussion:

1. If the 50 percent doesn't hold, what in Rabideau's chapter changes? Her case for introspection doesn't depend on the number. Her claim that a procedure fixed a field does.
2. The screen removes one signal at one moment. Name what it can't reach: who studied with whom, who could afford the travel and the months of preparation, who owns the instrument, who was encouraged to apply, and the final round after the screen comes down. If there's time, the sharpest version of that point is that the same orchestras that changed on gender did not change comparably on race, which is the gap Sphinx and Project STEP were built for.

## 8:00 Panic!

Brook, O'Brien and Taylor surveyed 2,487 people working in British culture in 2015, interviewed 237 of them, and set the results against four national datasets. The report puts what creative workers believe about their sector next to the measured composition of the workforce. The two don't match, and the mismatch is widest among the best paid.

### what they believe, Part 1, pp. 3–10

Asked what gets you ahead, 30 percent of respondents credited talent and hard work while rejecting class and connections, 34 percent named both merit and barriers, 21 percent emphasized barriers, 16 percent neither. The people most certain the sector rewards merit, and most doubtful that class, gender, or ethnicity matter, are those earning over £50,000, whatever their own origins (p. 7). Asked who they know, respondents knew other creatives and were unlikely to know factory workers, bus drivers, or postal workers (p. 9).

Read two passages back to back, p. 7 and p. 8: Kate, from a working-class background, putting her career down to tenacity, then Nisha, in film and television, saying what decides the work is who you know and who you've worked with. Ask which of them is describing the sector and which is describing herself.

### who's actually there, Part 2, pp. 11–19

Share Figure 3, p. 12.

- Black and minority ethnic workers: 2.7 percent in museums, galleries and libraries, 4.2 percent in film, TV and radio, 4.8 percent in music, performing and visual art.
- Working-class origins: 18.2 percent in music, performing and visual art, 12.6 percent in publishing. About a third of the population as a whole has those origins.
- Women earn an estimated £5,800 a year less than comparable men across the creative industries, about £15,000 less in film and TV (p. 15).

Then Figure 4, p. 17, which is the one to slow down on. Young cultural workers from upper-middle-class origins went from 15 percent in 1981 to 33 percent in 2011; those from working-class origins from 22 percent to 13 percent. The chances of getting in, by origin, held steady across those thirty years. What changed is Britain: in 1981 a person aged 19 to 28 was five times as likely to have working-class parents as upper-middle-class ones, and by 2011, one and a half times. A field can hold its practices constant and still become less representative.

### two mechanisms from Parts 3 and 4

You weren't asked to read these. About 87 percent of respondents had worked for free (p. 21), and the interviews split by class origin: Polly, from a middle-class family, calls unpaid work a choice, while Carla managed three months of it in London and calls the limit unfair (pp. 22–23). And hiring can be a form of cultural matching, where what a candidate is read for is shared taste (p. 33). Creative workers attend about four times as many art forms as people in working-class occupations. That makes the filter a narrow one.

Discussion:

1. The people most convinced the sector is fair are the people who set its hiring practices. What does that predict about how it changes? Press past "training" toward who controls the criteria.
2. Equal chances at entry, a workforce that got less representative anyway. Which of the two would a policy go after, and what would it have to do?
3. This is UK data. Which of it would you expect to hold in the American field you work in, and which wouldn't? London's concentration has an obvious counterpart in New York and Los Angeles. The class measure, based on parents' occupations, doesn't map onto how class is talked about here.

## 8:40 the Positionality and Access Statement

One to two pages, due Friday, September 18, by 11:59 pm on Canvas. It stays in the portfolio, and weeks 6 through 15 refer back to it.

The exercises tonight described a distribution. This component puts you in it. Positionality is your location in the field: the training, resources, relationships, and social identity that shape the access you have and the barriers you meet. The statement is your account of that location and what follows from it.

Two sentences, same writer:

- I've been lucky in some ways and have faced obstacles in others.
- A graduate degree from a school with an alumni network in the Bay Area puts me two emails from most new-music programmers here. It also means every reference I have works in one city, and the work I can point to is unpaid.

The second is what the brief asks for. Four things to cover:

- Position. Where you trained, where you live, what you own, what you owe, who you know, and whichever parts of your social identity you decide belong in the document.
- Access. Be as specific here as about the barriers: a network, a city with venues in it, an instrument already paid for, a language, family who can absorb a lean year, a teacher who made a call.
- Barriers. Name the mechanism rather than the feeling, using tonight's vocabulary.
- Consequences. What you plan around and what you push against, specific enough to change a line on your Career Ecosystem Map.

You decide what goes in it. Nothing here requires you to disclose anything you'd rather keep out of a document you submit, and there's no conclusion you're expected to reach.

Read it against your Creative Identity Statement and Values Statement before you submit. A contradiction between them is a revision item for the portfolio rather than a fault in either.
