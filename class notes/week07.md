### 6:05 week 6, the readings

The four readings share one claim: your audience isn't everyone.

Kolb, chapter 5 (pp. 104–127):

- A segment is a group of consumers who share what they want from a product. It has a need for the product, a desire to buy it, and the money to do so (p. 108).
- Market depth, "fishing out of the same pond": more customers from the same place, with the same traits and the same motivation as the ones you have (p. 106). The people who haven't bought have usually heard the message already. Her example is a custom-perfume maker sending more tweets to local college students who didn't respond the first time.
- Market breadth: a new segment that isn't buying yet. It gets its own message, and the existing segment keeps its own (p. 107).
- Four targeting strategies (p. 109):
  - undifferentiated: everyone wants the same thing ("everyone will love me")
  - concentrated: one segment, where most small businesses start
  - multi-segment: more than one segment at once, often the same product with a different message for each
  - microsegment: a small group with shared interests, found and reached through technology, even when they're far apart
- Three segmentation characteristics (pp. 112–113):
  - demographic: gender, age, ethnicity or nationality, income
  - geographic: location
  - psychographic: behavior, values, lifestyle
- Her starting point is one person who has bought something similar, described and then sorted into the three. Demographic and geographic segmentation is for designing the marketing. Psychographic segmentation is for developing the product (p. 113).
- Box 5.2 (p. 112), usage segmentation for the performing arts. A ticket is rarely an impulse buy. Three behaviors to track: which message prompted the purchase; whether the buyer is frequent, occasional, or new; whether videos of performances moved them to buy.

NYFA, chapter 11, section Understanding Your Audience:

- One real audience member, and five questions about that person:
  - who: the demographic facts that distinguish them
  - what: what they do, whether they're artists themselves, in an unrelated field, retired, in school
  - where: whether they live or work near you, your studio, the venue
  - when: when they came, and when they first learned of you
  - why: a fan of the genre, part of a social evening, new in town, there for the subject matter, there by accident
- How they heard: a friend (and which friend), a listing, the venue, a mailing list, social media, paid advertising. Then how they take in information in general, and which platforms and sources they trust.
- The composite sketch is a method from large companies. Artists often have more direct access to their audience, a small circle of supporters, fans, and clients.
- The same questions, repeated with other audience members, as different from each other as possible.

Landry, chapter 2, section Defining Your Audiences (pp. 29–37):

- Demographic traits are external and psychographic traits internal. She calls the psychographic "the most important, typically" (p. 30).
- Demographic (pp. 31–32): where they live, gender, age, education, income, single or married, raising kids or not.
- Psychographic (p. 32): place in the life cycle, priorities and dreams, beliefs, hobbies and interests, political leanings and religion, causes, activities and identity, occupation, personality and lifestyle, tastes in food, music, art, books, film, fashion, and shopping, worldview, how they like to feel, groups they belong to, aspirations.
- "Everyone will want my ___": "maybe. But probably not." Niche audiences with defined characteristics are "your best bet" (pp. 30–31).
- In the arts, a problem to solve often isn't the right frame. Needs and desires replace it: physical, emotional, spiritual, convenience, aspiration (p. 32).
- Survey response rates (p. 34): 10 percent is decent, 4 to 5 percent is the norm.

Andrews, chapter 3, sections The Exchange Model and Market Research (pp. 70–72):

- Marketing is an exchange that delivers value to each side. A venue sells a ticket: the patron gets the performance, and the musicians, staff, and owner get income and recognition (p. 70).
- The value can be something other than money. A web designer discounts a site for a new arts venture in return for access to its customer database (p. 70).
- His three questions (p. 71): how the customer perceives the value of what you offer, how the exchange can satisfy both sides, and how new customers can be reached through their needs and wants.
- Market research asks what customers are interested in, how they choose to engage with the arts, where they live, how they learn about new work, and what they treat as incentives or barriers (p. 71). It runs before launch, during the introduction, and once the venture is established.
- Before launch, similar businesses are a source, often through collaboration: galleries within walking distance organizing a shared Friday night. Without shared data, openings, performances, and receptions show what kinds of people come (p. 72).

### 6:35 Kolb chapter 3, the competitor

- Research is collecting and then analyzing data to answer a question. It's ongoing, before the plan and after the launch (p. 56).
- Her image is a map for a hike (p. 57). Hard terrain means more supplies and more time.
- Box 3.1 (pp. 55–56): Stephanie Ishman books musicians, singers, comedians, and poets, matching their talents to the benefits her clients want. The skills she names are cold calling, the legal language of contracts, and business database programs.

On screen, p. 57. Read aloud:

> The competitors that need to be researched are companies or organizations that provide a product with similar benefits that will meet the needs of the consumer. The product does not need to be a direct copy of what is being offered by the creative entrepreneur. While the creative individual will notice differences between closely related products, the average consumer may not. It is better for the creative entrepreneur to research competitors too widely than too narrowly.

- Four facts about each competitor (p. 57): the benefit, the price, how it's distributed, how it's promoted.
- What the four facts show: how your work is unique, its competitive advantage, and the needs "not met by their competitors" (pp. 57–58).
- Where they're found first (p. 58): websites, social media, newsletters, and what customers say on competitors' social media.
- The handcrafted chair (p. 58). One buyer wants a seat, another the style and color in the room, a third the memory of a person or an occasion. Marketing takes the seat for granted. Research looks for the other reasons.

To the room: what's the seat in your work, the benefit every competitor also provides? What are the other reasons someone chooses it?

Discussion:

1. A difference between your work and a nearby artist's that practitioners notice and an audience member wouldn't. A difference an audience member notices first. Which one do they choose on?
2. Artists network constantly and share ideas, and research "is just systemizing this process" (p. 64). Andrews, last week: similar organizations often learn about each other by collaborating. What changes when the competitor is someone you'd share a bill with?

### 6:55 exercise 1, the field around your audience

Outcome: a list of what your core audience member could choose instead of your work, reaching past your own discipline, with Kolb's four facts for three of the entries.

Worked example on screen, five minutes. It's invented.

A composer-performer runs a monthly electroacoustic concert in a small Oakland venue. The core person in the Audience Profile is in their late twenties to early forties, goes to small-venue shows a few times a month, follows experimental electronic artists online, and comes for the sound system and the room as much as for the program.

What that person could choose instead, in three rings:

- The same thing: other new-music and electroacoustic series in the East Bay and San Francisco, a university concert series with free admission.
- The same benefit in another form: an experimental club night at a bar, a listening bar playing records on a good system, an immersive-audio show at a planetarium.
- The same evening or the same money: a film at a repertory cinema, a friend's band, the album on headphones at home.

Kolb's four for three of them:

- University series. Benefit: new work, played well, in a seated hall. Price: free. Distribution: a campus hall, weeknights, with parking. Promotion: the department calendar and an email list.
- Club night. Benefit: a late night of sound on a big system among people like them. Price: a cover at the door. Distribution: a bar, 21 and over, starting at ten. Promotion: Instagram flyers and the DJs' own followings.
- Headphones at home. Benefit: the sound, alone, whenever. Price: a subscription they already pay. Distribution: anywhere. Promotion: the platform's recommendations.

To the room: which of these does the core person choose on most Fridays? What does the series offer that none of the three does? One answer: multichannel sound, live, in a room the size of a living room, with the composer at the desk, once a month.

Pairs, breakout rooms, fifteen minutes, seven each. In chat:

> Who is the core person in your Audience Profile? What could that person choose instead of your work, in three rings: the same thing, the same benefit in another form, the same evening or the same money? Ten entries or more. For three of them, one from each ring: what's the benefit, the price, where and how people get it, how it's promoted?

Back in the main room, in chat: the entry furthest from your own discipline.

Follow-ups:

- Which entries are colleagues?
- Which third-ring entries (free, at home, already paid for) take most of this person's evenings?
- Where are the four facts still missing?

The Reflection asks for two or three others offering a similar benefit to a similar audience.

### 7:40 Kolb chapter 3, research and bias

Five steps (p. 59):

1. Write the research question: what do we need to know?
2. Decide on the source: whom should we ask?
3. Choose the method: what tool?
4. Conduct the research: when, where, and by whom?
5. Analyze the findings: what do the answers tell us?

The research question (pp. 59–61):

- The topic she calls most critical is the benefit the customer wants from the work (p. 59).
- Questions narrow across rewrites (p. 60). "How should I price my product?" becomes "What price are middle-class mothers willing to pay for a handcrafted lunch bag for children?" "Why do people buy mass-produced prints?" becomes "Why do professionally employed women, aged 25–35, buy prints from discount stores to decorate their offices?"
- It takes several attempts, and it goes best with two people challenging each other over what's being asked (p. 60).
- Two of her samples (pp. 60–61). Competitor: "What new products have our closest competitors introduced during the last year?" Promotion: "What social media do our current customers aged 45 and older use at least weekly?"
- The first word decides the method (p. 61). Who, what, how many, and how often have a limited set of answers and go to a survey. Why goes to focus groups, interviews, and observation.

Sources (pp. 61–67):

- Secondary data comes first: data someone else collected, cheaper and faster. Government data such as the census, academic and trade association research, lifestyle publications and social media (pp. 61–62). For arts participation in the US, the National Endowment for the Arts runs the Survey of Public Participation in the Arts.
- Box 3.2 (p. 63), secondary data at scale. Deloitte's 2023 survey of more than 22,000 young people in 44 countries: almost half feel burned out at work, half live paycheck to paycheck, they expect companies to make the world better, and more than half say social media makes them feel lonely or inadequate.
- Primary data from people you know is biased: your friends probably share your preferences (p. 64).
- The population can be too large (every woman in New York City who buys handcrafted jewelry) or too small (everyone over 70 in Berlin who takes ceramics classes) (p. 65).
- For interviews and focus groups: the convenience method (people who fit the profile and will say yes) and the snowball method (those people recruit others like them) (p. 66).

Methods (pp. 68–74):

- surveys: paper, email, social media, website, text
- focus groups: group dynamics draw out responses. Her example (p. 71): people avoid a downtown gallery because the area is "scary," and asking each of them what they mean turns up the trash on the street. The moderator shouldn't be the artist, whose defensiveness stops people from talking.
- interviews: one-to-one, in depth, with few participants chosen for what they know
- intercept interviews: quick questions on the street
- observation: watching behavior where it happens, in the gallery for gallery visitors

On screen, Box 3.3, p. 74. Three searches for this week:

- the names of competing artists and organizations
- what competitors' customers say on their social media
- competitors' marketing messages

Three biases (pp. 77–78):

- Confirmation bias: attention to the results you hoped for, and not to the negative ones.
- Irrational exuberance, in her definition: irrational decisions made on the strength of rational decisions made earlier. Building a prototype was a reasonable call, and the survey gets read as a reason to keep going.
- Social desirability bias sits with the person answering. They say they'd pay more for work with a social benefit, and they buy the cheaper one.

Discussion:

1. An entrepreneur who does this research is two and a half times as likely to start a business successfully (p. 56). The page gives no source. What would the number have to compare before you'd trust it? Follow-up: who chooses to do research in the first place?
2. Which of the three biases is likeliest in your Audience Profile? Confirmation bias in whom you asked, social desirability in what your friends told you, irrational exuberance in the project you've already spent a year on.

### 8:00 NYFA, your place in the market

NYFA, chapter 11, section Understanding Your Work and Your Place in the Market.

The work:

- what you do, and the material or instrument
- the subject; its music example is the music of a particular time and place, "like French Baroque or Klezmer"
- size, how long it lasts, what conditions it needs to exist

How work like yours is consumed:

- in spaces dedicated to art, or in public space
- by ticket, or by purchase
- as a single object or a limited run, or made for large-scale consumption, the way music, literature, and media usually are

"Taken collectively, all of these factors help determine your place in the market."

The definition of a market. Read aloud:

> the group of consumers or organizations that is interested in the product [or service], has the resources to purchase the product [or service], and is permitted by law and other regulations to acquire the product [or service].

Three conditions: interest, resources, permission.

A place within a market:

- Cars divide by price, safety, fuel efficiency, size, recreational use, and aesthetics. A plan for a sport utility vehicle differs from one for a self-driving car.
- Neighboring markets hold reference points. Wearable art sits in fashion and luxury as well as art. A musician who plays weddings researches the wedding industry.
- Market data also shows what artists in the same segment are doing, whom they sell to, and how they market.

Discussion:

1. Interest, resources, permission. Which one sets the limit of your market? Permission: an age limit at the door, a venue's license, a school district's approval for a residency. Resources, as in week 5: the ticket price and a free evening decide who's in the market before interest does.
2. The car market has axes. What two axes fit the market your work is in, and where do you sit on each? If time allows, the two axes go on the Zoom whiteboard with a few entries from exercise 1 placed on them.
3. What's your wedding industry, a neighboring market where your work also sells, with its own data and its own competitors? Film and game audio, music education, sound installation for museums and architecture.

### 8:15 Landry, the unique value

Landry, chapter 1, pp. 14–16, from the chapter read in week 3.

- The Unique Value Principle is "why a customer is drawn to you" (p. 14). It "acts like a magnet between your products and services as opposed to anyone else" (pp. 14–15).
- On identity (p. 14): in business the UVP attaches to what is sold, "but it stems from the creator." When a company or an artist has trouble gaining traction, it can go back to uncertainty about who they are and what makes them special.
- The UVP and the mission are related, and she develops them together (p. 14). The Mission Statement is from week 3, the Creative Identity Statement from week 1.

Read aloud, p. 15:

> The UVP communicates your difference compared to someone with a similar offering or skill set. It may take a while to pinpoint this and requires looking at your competition for comparison sake. Often this is a blend of your energy, your vibe, your aesthetic, values, beliefs, and worldview together.

Her word-cloud list (p. 15):

- your creative abilities
- your professional abilities
- your related skills
- your random talents
- what you love doing
- what you do well
- what people always ask you for help with
- how you help people
- the emotions you want your work to evoke
- a product or service you could sell
- the sounds, smells, tools, colors, and materials of your work

The word cloud or a mind map (pp. 15–17) narrows to "a short phrase."

Discussion:

1. Every item on the list describes the maker. Kolb, p. 57: the audience may not see the differences the maker sees. With the core person from your Audience Profile in mind, which items would that person notice, and which would only you?
2. "What people always ask you for help with" comes from other people. What do people ask you for? That's a value somebody already chooses you for.

A position is a difference that's yours (Landry's half) and that your audience chooses you for (Kolb's half).

### 8:30 exercise 2, the research question

Outcome: one research question, rewritten until it says exactly what you need to know and exactly whom you'd ask, with a method for answering it.

Worked on screen, three minutes, from the exercise 1 example:

- Draft 1: Would people come to my concerts?
- Draft 2: Why do people go to experimental music nights?
- Draft 3: Why do people aged 25–40 who came to at least two of the series' concerts this year choose it over a club night on the same Friday?
- Method: draft 3 starts with why, so interviews. Five people from the series' email list rather than the composer's friends (p. 64), asked by someone other than the composer, following Kolb's advice for focus groups (p. 71).

Same pairs, breakout rooms, fifteen minutes, seven each. In chat:

> Which line in your Audience Profile is inferred, or which difference from exercise 1 would your audience choose you for? As a research question, what is it? After your partner's rewrite, what exactly do you need to know, and exactly whom would you ask? After one more rewrite: survey (who, what, how many, how often), or interviews and observation (why)?

Back in the main room, in chat: the final drafts.

Follow-ups:

- Whom would you ask, and how many of them are your friends?
- With a survey, at Landry's 4 to 5 percent response rate (p. 34), how many people have to receive it?
