## weeks 1 and 2

Week 1 took entrepreneurship away from money-making. In these four books it means seeing an opportunity and building something from the skills and resources you already have, and money is the means that buys back the time to work.

- Kolb put two questions in front of you: sell through an intermediary or directly, and build a nonprofit or a business.
- Andrews builds a venture from your values rather than from a product, and separates a lifetime practice from a venture built to be sold.
- Rabideau treats the artist and the entrepreneur as the same animal: five mindsets, three skill sets.
- McCurdy is blunt about the economy you're entering and starts from the question of who you are.

Week 2 went underneath identity, to voice and values, and all three chapters treat both as discovered rather than declared.

- McCurdy sends you into your own roots for the story only you can tell.
- Rabideau's strengths chapter puts you past your instrument, with Sarah Robinson's internal compass as the model: a short set of questions she checks real work against.
- The creativity chapter puts your voice where predictability stops.

One idea from week 2 carries into tonight. A stated value is the one that goes on a website. A governing value has turned something down.

The sequence so far: your identity says who you are, your values say how you choose, your mission says what you do now and for whom, your vision says where you're going.

## the two components you've written

### Creative Identity Statement

First person, half a page to a page, who you are as an artist and the work you do, readable by a stranger who's seen nothing else of yours. Every component after it refers back to the artist described there.

Check yours for these:

- The actual practice is named: instruments, media, settings, collaborators, and what the work is after.
- Your position right now is stated, at whatever stage you're actually at.
- It describes the work rather than claiming something about its quality.
- The question of what you make gets answered rather than deferred behind a position about identity being unnameable.
- A list of activities reads as a description of a practice rather than as evidence that you're hard to categorize.
- The work is named by what it is, not by adjectives.

Uncertainty about direction belongs in this statement. Uncertainty about what you do is the part to fix, and two or three sentences saying what you make and where you work will fix it.

### Values Statement

Two or three values, each with what it means in your practice and how it would guide a real decision. Mission and vision get built on top of this.

Check yours for these:

- A cost is attached: you name what you protect when protecting it is expensive, and what work you decline.
- Each value is applied to a decision rather than described.
- The example is something that happened rather than something hypothetical.

The test, and you'll run it tonight: hand the statement a concrete opportunity and see whether it gives you an answer.

## the readings

### part one, the definitions

All four chapters define the same pair. A mission is present purpose: what you do, for whom, and why. A vision is future direction, or your values held up as the lens every decision passes through.

- NYFA: a mission in under two dozen words, and a vision that's either a future goal or your nonnegotiables.
- Kolb: a mission broken into who you are, what you do, and for whom, with values as the how.
- Andrews: five questions that build a mission, what we do, why, how, for whom, and what benefit, plus a before-and-after that sharpens a vague one.
- Landry: the mission as a mantra, surfaced from a Word Cloud or mind map and from what makes your work distinct.

### part two, the statements as a filter

All four make the same claim: a mission and a vision are useful when they decide something. NYFA's grant fits neither statement and gets declined even though it brings money. Andrews's community arts organization turns down a controversial sponsor. Kolb calls the pair a compass. Landry's mantra doubles as a filter for which clients and activities to take.

Bring the opportunity your statements would decline. If nothing would be declined, we'll look at why.

Two places we'll press:

- Landry lets mission and vision blur when you're working on a single project. NYFA, Kolb, and Andrews keep them apart. When you write one sentence, which of the two are you writing?
- Kolb splits your personal statements from your business ones and keeps the values consistent across both. That's live for this room: teaching, contract, and studio income sitting alongside the creative practice. Kolb calls this the portfolio career, several revenue streams running at once, and it's the frame for the Career Ecosystem Map you build next week.

## exercises

### exercise 1, the decision test

Pairs, breakout rooms, twelve minutes.

1. One of you reads your Values Statement aloud.
2. The other puts a concrete opportunity to it: a commission whose deadline eats your summer, a well-paid gig for a client whose politics you dislike, a teaching job in another state, a collaboration with someone whose work you find thin.
3. Decide together whether the statement answers, or whether you had to reach past it.
4. Switch.

Back in the main room we'll take three or four cases where the statement couldn't decide. Watch for values pitched at a level of generality that would fit anyone in the room.

### exercise 2, the mission in under two dozen words

Seven minutes of silent writing, then NYFA's verbal test.

1. Write a mission in under twenty-four words: what you do, for whom, and why.
2. Four or five of you read yours aloud, once, camera on.
3. Everyone else writes down the words that stuck, then posts them in chat.
4. Compare the words that stuck with what you meant to say.

Watch for art-speak, for a mission that describes a feeling rather than an activity, and for one that would fit any musician here.

### exercise 3, the conflict round

Twenty-five minutes, from the reflection you submitted before class.

1. One sentence each: where your present purpose and your future direction don't line up. Nobody solves anything during the round.
2. We pick two to work: one where the mission is too narrow for the vision, one where the vision needs income the mission doesn't produce.
3. Ten minutes on each, as a group. What comes out is a sharper statement of the conflict.

Shapes that come up: the practice that pays against the practice you want to build, a vision naming an audience the mission's activity can't reach, a vision that's a job title.
