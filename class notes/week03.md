## weeks 1 and 2, the through-line

Week 1 took entrepreneurship away from money-making: seeing an opportunity and building something from the skills and resources already in hand, with money as the means that buys back time to work. Kolb put two questions in front of them, intermediary or direct and nonprofit or business. Andrews built the venture from values rather than from a product, and separated a lifetime practice from a venture built to be sold. Rabideau treated the artist and the entrepreneur as the same animal, five mindsets and three skill sets. McCurdy was blunt about the economy and started from the question of who you are.

Week 2 went underneath identity to voice and values, both discovered rather than declared. McCurdy sent them into their own roots for the story only they can tell. Rabideau's strengths chapter reframed them past their instrument, with Sarah Robinson's internal compass as the model: a short set of questions checked against real work. The creativity chapter put voice where predictability stops.

The line into tonight: the identity says who, the values say how you choose, the mission says what you do now and for whom, the vision says where you are going. Each component narrows the one before it into something that can decide an actual question. Say this once at the top and come back to it in each exercise.

Also from week 2 and worth restating tonight: the gap between stated values and governing values. A stated value is the one that goes on a website. A governing value has turned something down.

## the two components they have written

### Creative Identity Statement (week 1)

First person, half a page to a page, who they are as an artist and the work they do, readable by a stranger who has seen nothing else of theirs. Every later component refers back to the artist described there.

A working one names the actual practice: the instruments, media, settings, collaborators, and the concern the work is after. It states where the artist is right now, at whatever stage that is. It reads as description rather than as a claim about quality.

Where drafts fall short: the question of what they make gets deferred behind a position about identity being unnameable; a list of activities appears as evidence of being hard to categorize rather than as a description of a practice; adjectives stand in for the work; the current position is stated as an aspiration or left out. Uncertainty about direction is not a fault here. Uncertainty about what they do is.

The revision ask, when it comes up tonight: two or three sentences that say what they make and where they work.

### Values Statement (week 2)

Two or three values, each with what it means in their practice and how it would guide a real decision. This is the filter that mission and vision get built on top of.

A working one attaches a cost. It names what gets protected when protecting it is expensive, and what work gets declined. Values nobody would dispute, held without a decision attached, do not filter anything.

Where drafts fall short: the value is named and then described rather than applied; the example is hypothetical; the whole set is aspirational.

The test to use out loud: hand the statement a concrete opportunity and see whether it returns an answer.

## reading discussion

Full summaries in the reading notes. Tonight:

Part one, the definitions. All four chapters define the same pair. Mission is present purpose: what you do, for whom, why. Vision is future direction, or the values held up as the lens decisions pass through. Get the four definitions on the screen side by side and ask what the differences cost. Put NYFA's under-two-dozen-words constraint and Kolb's who / what / for-whom breakdown to them as tests to write against.

Part two, the statements as filter. NYFA's grant that fits neither statement, Andrews's community arts organization declining the controversial sponsor, Kolb's compass, Landry's mantra that doubles as a filter for which clients to take. Ask each of them for the opportunity their statements would decline. Push on the ones where the answer is that nothing would be declined.

Two places to press:

Landry lets mission and vision blur for a single project, where NYFA, Kolb, and Andrews keep them distinct. Ask which of the two they are actually writing when they write one sentence.

Kolb's split between personal statements and business statements, with values consistent across both, is live for this group: teaching, contract, and studio income sitting alongside the creative practice. Kolb's portfolio career is the frame for week 4's Career Ecosystem Map, so name the term tonight.

## exercises

### exercise 1, the decision test

Pairs, breakout rooms, twelve minutes total.

1. One reads their Values Statement aloud.
2. The partner puts a concrete opportunity to it: a commission with a deadline that eats the summer, a well-paid gig for a client whose politics they dislike, a teaching job in another state, a collaboration with someone whose work they find thin.
3. The pair decides whether the statement answers, or whether they had to reach past it.
4. Switch.

Back in the main room, take three or four reports of a statement that could not decide. Listen for values stated at a level of generality that fits anyone.

### exercise 2, the mission in under two dozen words

Silent writing, seven minutes, then the verbal test from NYFA.

1. Each writes a mission in under twenty-four words, answering what, for whom, and why.
2. Four or five read theirs aloud, once, with cameras on.
3. Listeners write down the words that stuck, then post them in chat.
4. Compare what stuck against what the writer meant to say.

Listen for art-speak, for missions that describe a feeling rather than an activity, and for the ones that would fit any musician in the room.

### exercise 3, the conflict round

From the reflection they submitted before class. Twenty-five minutes.

1. Round-robin, one sentence each: where the present purpose and the future direction do not line up. No solving during the round.
2. Pick two conflicts the group can work: one where the mission is too narrow for the vision, one where the vision requires income the mission does not produce.
3. Work each for ten minutes as a group. The outcome is a sharper statement of the conflict rather than a resolution.

Common shapes: the practice that pays conflicts with the practice they want to build; the vision names a scale of audience the mission's activity cannot reach; the vision is a job title rather than a direction.
