Business Models for Artists

Movement III: business model and money

Movement III turns to money, and it starts with the whole machine rather than any one part: how your practice creates, delivers, and captures value, held on one page.

reading · NYFA 2, Kolb 7, Rabideau 8, Landry 5 reflection due · Mon Oct 19, before class component due · Fri Oct 23, Canvas

this week

A business model is how a practice creates, delivers, and captures value: what you offer and to whom, how it reaches them, and how the money comes back. The tool for holding all of that on one page is the business model canvas, a grid that maps a venture into a handful of connected blocks.

Rabideau puts the value proposition at the center and gives a usable template: a named thing that, through its distinctive attributes, provides particular benefits to a particular audience. That is the create-value core, and everything else on the canvas serves it. Kolb's distribution chapter is the deliver-value part: how your offering actually reaches people, directly (your studio, your site, a pop-up) or through an intermediary (an agent, a label, a marketplace) that reaches them for a cut. Landry is the capture-value part: a business model, stripped down, is how you generate revenue and what it costs, or in Guy Kawasaki's blunter version, who has your money and how you get it into your pocket.

NYFA supplies the warning that keeps this honest. A canvas is a set of hypotheses, not a finished plan, since no plan survives first contact with customers: you fill it in, then test it against real people and adjust. Read for where your own model is a guess, and where it is fragile. Most creative practices run on more than one revenue stream, and the useful question is not only how you make money but what happens when one stream dries up.

the reading

Four readings, one per side of the model plus the method that holds it together. Landry is read for its revenue sections; the rest are full chapters.

NYFA, ch. 2, "The Business Model Canvas and Lean Methodology" (pp. 34–51)

The canvas itself: Osterwalder's one-page map of a venture, and the Lean method behind it, fill in your hypotheses, then test them against real people.

look for · the blocks of the canvas, and the idea that no plan survives first contact with customers.

Kolb, ch. 7, "Distributing your product" (pp. 151–174)

The deliver-value part: how your offering reaches your audience, directly or through an intermediary, and how intensively you distribute it.

look for · the trade-off between direct and indirect channels, and what an intermediary takes in exchange for reach.

Rabideau, ch. 8, "The Business Side: Laying the Groundwork" (pp. 85–100)

The canvas from an artist's side, built around the value proposition, with a template for writing one and the mindset to carry into it.

look for · the value-proposition format, and the point that it guides every other part of the canvas.

Landry, ch. 5, "Generate and Launch" (pp. 109–128)

Read the revenue-and-business-model sections: revenue versus profit, and a business model reduced to how you get paid and what it costs.

look for · the two basic parts of a business model, and Kawasaki's blunt version of the question.

Business Model Snapshot

A Business Model Snapshot maps, on one page, how your practice creates, delivers, and captures value. It is a canvas (a grid or a structured one-pager) rather than an essay, and it pulls your earlier components into a working whole: your value proposition at the center, the audience it serves, how you reach them, and how the money comes back.

Questions to build it from:

  • Create: what is your value proposition, what you offer, to whom, and how they benefit?
  • Deliver: how does your offering reach your audience, directly or through an intermediary?
  • Capture: how do you make money, and across which revenue streams?
  • Costs: what does it cost to create and deliver, the essential expenses?
  • Fragility: which stream are you most dependent on, and where is the model thin?

Build a Business Model Snapshot on one page: your value proposition, the audience it serves, how you deliver it, your revenue streams, and your main costs. Use a canvas format, a grid or a structured one-pager, rather than prose, and mark where the model is fragile. Submit it on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday.
