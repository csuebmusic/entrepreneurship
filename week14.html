Financial Sustainability & Funding

Movement V: legal and financial sustainability

This is an asynchronous week, with no Monday meeting for Fall Recess, so you pace the work yourself. It asks two plain questions: what does it cost to keep your practice going, and where does the money come from?

this week

Sustaining a practice comes down to money in and money out. A budget, in NYFA's terms, is an annual picture of all your income set against all your costs, and the financial statements NYFA and Kolb describe (chiefly the income statement, revenue minus expenses) tell you whether the practice is actually sustaining itself or only feeling like it is. Kolb adds the discipline that keeps this honest: separate your business finances from your personal ones, so you can see what the practice earns and spends on its own terms, and not mistake activity for profit.

The second question is funding, and the key idea across the readings is that most practices run on a portfolio of sources rather than one. Rabideau splits them into what you earn (sales, tickets, fees) and what you raise (grants, donations, sponsorship, crowdfunding, membership), and warns that not every source fits every project: your status and focus decide which doors are open. Grants in particular reward fit and preparation, so you research whether your work matches the funder and give the application real time.

Read for a realistic year. The reflection asks what sustaining your practice for a year would actually take, and where the funding could come from, and the snapshot turns that into a picture you can hold.

the reading

Four readings across the two questions: money in and out, and where the funding comes from.

NYFA, ch. 6, "Budgets" (pp. 109–116)

Why budgets matter most for small practices, and the two basic kinds: the project budget and the annual budget (all income against all costs).

look for · the annual budget as the money-in, money-out picture of a year.

NYFA, ch. 7, "Financial Terminology" (pp. 117–135)

The vocabulary of financial health: the income statement, the balance sheet, and taxes.

look for · the income statement (revenue minus expenses) as the basic read on whether a practice is sustaining itself.

Rabideau, ch. 11, "Finding the Funds: Donations, Grants, and Finance Management" (pp. 133–146)

Building a realistic budget and securing revenue from a portfolio of sources, with a practical guide to grants.

look for · the split between earned and raised revenue, and the point that not every funding source fits every project.

Kolb, ch. 10, "Financial concepts for success" (pp. 224–248)

Financial management for a working practice: reading statements, managing cash flow, and separating business from personal finances.

look for · the case for separate accounts, and not mistaking activity for profit.

portfolio component due Fri Nov 27 · Canvas

Financial Snapshot

A Financial Snapshot is a simple picture of the money moving through your practice, what comes in and what goes out over a realistic year, together with a funding path: the mix of earned and raised sources that could sustain it. It builds on your Business Model Snapshot and Pricing Rationale, and it should be a clear picture rather than a full accounting.

Questions to build it from:

  • What would a realistic year cost: your main expenses, fixed and variable?
  • What income does the practice bring in now, and from which sources?
  • Where is the gap between what it costs and what it earns?
  • What is your funding path: which mix of earned (sales, fees) and raised (grants, donations, crowdfunding) sources fits your work?
  • Which of those sources actually fit your status and focus, and which are a poor match?

Build a Financial Snapshot: the money in and out of your practice over a realistic year, and a funding path (the mix of earned and raised sources) fitted to it. Keep it a clear picture rather than a full accounting. Submit it on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday.
