Power, Social Identity & Access

Module I: orientation, identity, and the power lens

The first four weeks looked inward and then outward: who you are, and the field your work moves through. This week looks at the forces that decide who reaches that field and who does not.

reading · Rabideau 7 and two outside readings reflection due · Mon Sep 14, before class component due · Fri Sep 18, Canvas

this week

Power and social identity, race, gender, class, and more, have shaped who gets to build a career in the arts, and still do. The material can feel uncomfortable to sit with. Rabideau opens his own chapter by saying so, and asking you to stay with the discomfort. The aim here is honest thinking grounded in evidence, not a position you are expected to arrive at.

Rabideau starts at the level of the individual: implicit bias, the assumptions we act on without noticing, and the work of building teams where every voice is heard. His clearest case is the blind audition, where a screen between the musician and the jury raised the share of women in top orchestras. The two outside readings widen the lens from bias to structure. Goldin and Rouse are the economists behind that blind-audition finding, and their evidence has been argued over since, which is worth reading for. The Panic! report carries the question from music to the creative industries at large, and from gender to class, with the numbers on who actually gets in.

Read for two things. First, the mechanisms: how access gets distributed, through gatekeepers, through who knows whom, through unpaid work, through whose taste counts as taste. Second, your own place in it. Each of us enters the field from somewhere, carrying some access and meeting some barriers.

the reading

One chapter from Rabideau and two outside readings, posted on Canvas and open access at the links below. The outside readings are substantial, so focus on the sections noted rather than every page.

Rabideau, ch. 7, "Diversity and Inclusivity: Abandoning Bias and Listening to Voices Unlike Our Own" (pp. 73–84)

The individual lens: what diversity and inclusion mean, how implicit bias works and how it can be unlearned, and how access organizations open a field. Written by an HR executive, and invitational in tone.

look for · the blind-audition case, and the turn to introspection: whom you seek out, and the patterns you might not notice.

Goldin & Rouse (2000), "Orchestrating Impartiality: The Impact of 'Blind' Auditions on Female Musicians," American Economic Review 90(4), 715–741

The study behind Rabideau's example. Screens raised a woman's odds of advancing by about half and help explain the rise of women in US orchestras since 1970. Open access via NBER. Focus on the introduction and results.

look for · the size of the screen's effect, and the reasons its causal claim has been contested since.

Brook, O'Brien & Taylor (2018), "Panic! Social Class, Taste and Inequalities in the Creative Industries," Create London

The widest lens: the first sociological study of social mobility into creative work, on how class, gender, and race shape who gets in across the arts. It sets the belief that culture is a meritocracy against the workforce numbers. Open access via Create London. Focus on the introduction and Parts 1 and 2.

look for · who most believes the sector is fair, and the role of networks and unpaid work in keeping it closed.

portfolio component due Fri Sep 18 · Canvas

Positionality and Access Statement

Positionality is your location in the field: the parts of your identity and circumstance, your background, training, resources, and social identity, that shape the access you have and the barriers you meet. A Positionality and Access Statement is a candid account of where you stand: the access your position carries, the barriers it carries, and what both mean for the framework you are building. It stays in the portfolio as a durable anchor you can return to as your framework develops. There is no required conclusion. The aim is an honest map of where you stand, in your own terms.

Questions to work from:

  • What is your position in your field: your background, training, resources, and the social identities you carry into it?
  • What access does that position give you, the doors that are, realistically, more open to you?
  • What barriers does it carry, the ones the readings help you name?
  • Where do the mechanisms from the readings, gatekeeping, networks, unpaid work, whose taste counts, touch your own path?
  • What does this mean for the framework you are building: what will you plan around, or push against?

Write a candid Positionality and Access Statement of one to two pages: where you stand in your field, the access and barriers your position carries, and what that means for the framework you are building. Submit it on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday.
