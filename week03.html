Mission & Vision

Module I: orientation, identity, and the power lens

This is an asynchronous week, with no Monday meeting for Labor Day, so you pace the work yourself. It is also where identity and values turn into direction: a mission for what you do now, and a vision for where you are going.

this week

A mission says what you do, for whom, and why, in the present. A vision says where you are going: the change you work toward, or the values you will not sacrifice. Together they are the foundation your planning rests on, so this week turns the identity and values you have named into a direction.

All four readings are planning chapters, and they agree more than they differ. NYFA and Andrews build a mission by answering plain questions (what, for whom, why, and what benefit) and a vision out of your values, the lens you filter decisions through. Kolb frames the pair as a compass: the mission is your purpose now, the vision is what you want to become, and every real decision should point back to them. Landry is the loosest and most playful, treating the mission as a mantra you could hang on a wall. Watch where they part: NYFA, Kolb, and Andrews keep mission and vision distinct, while Landry lets them blur for a single project.

The test of a good mission and vision is not how they sound but what they decide. Each reading makes the same move: when an opportunity arrives, a grant, a gig, a collaboration, you hold it up against your mission and vision, and if it pulls you away from them, you decline it. That only works if the statements are short and honest, closer to a sentence than a paragraph, and in your own words rather than art-speak.

the reading

Four planning chapters, one from each of NYFA, Kolb, Landry, and Andrews. They cover the same ground from a clean-definitions angle, a compass angle, a playful angle, and a thorough one. Read them in any order.

NYFA, ch. 1, "Strategic Planning for Artists" (pp. 18–33)

The anchor. NYFA defines mission and vision cleanly, treats the vision as your nonnegotiables and the lens decisions pass through, and then walks from there into an environmental scan and a SWOT analysis. Exercise-driven.

look for · the mission and vision definitions and Exercise 1.1, and the grant example where the two statements act as a litmus test.

Kolb, ch. 2, "Making the dream a reality" (pp. 31–54)

Mission, vision, and values as the compass for every strategic decision, plus the portfolio career, the idea that most artists run more than one revenue stream. Note the split between your personal statements and your business ones.

look for · his who / what / for-whom breakdown of a mission, the sample mission statements, and the line that the statements are a compass.

Landry, ch. 1, "Plan" (pp. 1–20)

The playful, self-directed take, and a good fit for an independent week: daydreaming with purpose, a Word Cloud and a mind map to surface what makes your work distinct, and the mission written as a short mantra.

look for · the mission-statement exercise and examples, and the point where Landry lets mission and vision blur.

Andrews, ch. 2, "Planning and Assessment" (pp. 35–66)

The most thorough of the four: the five questions that build a mission, a before-and-after that sharpens a vague one, and the SWOT that follows. It carries the planning arc end to end.

look for · the five mission questions, the Theatrical Theater before and after, and the mission used to turn down a sponsorship.

Mission Statement and Vision Statement

This week you write two components. A Mission Statement says what you do, for whom, and why, in the present. A Vision Statement says where you are going: the change you work toward, or the values you will not sacrifice. They build on your Creative Identity Statement and Values Statement.

For the mission, work from these:

  • What do you do, and for whom?
  • Why does it matter: what are you offering, or trying to change?
  • What would a stranger understand about your practice from one reading?

For the vision:

  • What do you want your practice to become, a few years out?
  • What change do you work toward, or what will you not sacrifice under any circumstances?
  • Where do you want to be, and for whom, that you are not yet?

Write a Mission Statement and a Vision Statement, each a sentence or two, in plain language rather than art-speak. Keep both short enough to act as a filter: you could hold a real opportunity up against them and decide. Submit both on Canvas by 11:59 pm Friday.
