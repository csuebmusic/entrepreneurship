# week 13 reading notes

Legal & Professional Issues, the first week of Module V. Reflection only, no portfolio component. Four legal chapters, and the aim is legal awareness rather than legal advice: know the concepts, protect your work, and know when to bring in a professional. This is the most fact-sensitive week, so the summaries stay to what the readings actually state. Drawn from the chapters.

## NYFA, ch. 3, "Intellectual Property" (pp. 55–74)

The rights chapter. NYFA separates the four kinds of intellectual property: copyright (original works of authorship), trademark and service mark (brand names and logos), patent (inventions), and trade secret, all grounded in federal law with international treaty protection. The move that matters most is the distinction between a copy and the rights: legally acquiring a song lets you use that copy personally, but the rights to reproduce, distribute, alter, or perform it publicly remain with the creators (songwriters, publishers, producers, label). NYFA frames copyright's purpose as incentivizing creation by letting creators benefit economically and control use, and stresses that the same understanding works both ways, to protect your own work and to use others' work without infringing.

key ideas: the four IP types; owning a copy is not owning the rights; protect your work and avoid infringing others'.

## NYFA, ch. 4, "Contracts" (pp. 75–82)

The agreements chapter. NYFA calls a contract a road map to a relationship: a meeting of the minds that records who is involved, for what purpose, who owes what, what is exchanged, how long it lasts, who owns the resulting physical and intellectual property, and what remedies apply if someone breaches. Contracts can be written or verbal, formal or informal, and are enforceable if not egregious or illegal, but the practical counsel is to put agreements in writing. The Statute of Frauds requires certain agreements to be written (among them anything that cannot be completed within a year, real-estate sales, sales of goods worth $500 or more, and work-made-for-hire), and the parties must have legal capacity to contract in the first place.

key ideas: a contract as a road map recording obligations and ownership; get it in writing; some agreements the law requires in writing.

## NYFA, ch. 5, "Business Entities" (pp. 83–103)

The structures chapter. NYFA walks the legal forms a venture can take, sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation, limited liability company, the social-enterprise forms (benefit corporation and L3C), and the not-for-profit, and organizes the choice around two axes: liability and tax. Its sharpest point is about the sole proprietorship: it is the simplest to start and to report taxes for, but personal liability is unrestricted, so a lost suit or unpaid debt can reach any personal asset, including a home, car, and savings. It repeatedly urges confirming your specific situation with an accountant or the IRS.

key ideas: the range of business structures; liability and tax as the deciding axes; the sole proprietor's unlimited personal exposure; confirm with a professional.

## Andrews, ch. 5, "Legal Issues" (pp. 145–177)

The wider legal overview, and it opens with a disclaimer that the chapter is a general overview, not legal advice, which should come from a qualified attorney. From there it covers choosing a corporate form (the for-profit versus not-for-profit decision and its consequences for tax, filing, and raising money; sole proprietorship, LLC, and the 501(c)(3) route with a fiscal sponsor), artists' rights under the law, tax planning, limiting liability through insurance and risk management, and the governance apparatus of a formal organization (board of directors, articles of incorporation, bylaws, memorandums of understanding).

key ideas: the not-legal-advice disclaimer; corporate form and its tax and fundraising consequences; artists' rights; insurance and risk; governance.

## how they fit together

The four cover the legal ground a working artist stands on: intellectual property and copyright (NYFA 3), contracts (NYFA 4), the structure of the venture itself (NYFA 5 and Andrews 5, both organized around liability and tax), and artists' rights and risk (Andrews 5). The consistent lesson, stated outright by both NYFA and Andrews, is that these are overviews rather than advice for any particular situation, so the professional skill is knowing the concepts well enough to spot an issue, ask the right questions, and recognize when to bring in a lawyer or accountant. There is no portfolio component this week (outcome 2). The reflection is the week's work: take an ethics-and-legal case from the readings and reason it through, how you would handle it and what you would need to find out.
