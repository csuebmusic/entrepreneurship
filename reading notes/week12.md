# week 12 reading notes

Social Media, Platforms & Relationships, the last week of Module IV, and the point where the graduate "implement" verb applies: the marketing work becomes a concrete, relational plan. Four mostly-new readings. Summaries drawn from the chapters.

## Kolb, ch. 9, "Building social media relationships" (pp. 198–222)

The relationships frame. Kolb argues social media is not just another broadcast channel and sorts a venture's presence into three kinds of media: paid (ads the company buys and fully controls, which build awareness but guarantee nothing), owned (the company's own sites, feeds, blogs, and platforms), and earned (other people talking about the work, through reviews, bloggers, and independent communities, which the company can monitor and respond to but not control). His key point is that owned media is where relationships form, because it is two-way: it invites the public to respond, starts conversations, and takes more time and energy than paid media precisely because you answer back, which is what builds trust and community. He also warns against abandoning traditional media, since no one is online all the time, and recommends integrating the two.

key ideas: paid, owned, and earned media; owned media as the two-way, relationship-building channel; integrate rather than broadcast.

## NYFA, ch. 12, "Marketing Tools and Resources" (pp. 182–205)

The practical tools. NYFA turns from the theory of earlier chapters to the nuts and bolts of making marketing materials and getting them in front of an audience, and treats social media as the single most important tool now available to artists, since digital channels erased the old cost barriers to reaching a wide audience. Its central lesson is fit: each platform has its own style, culture, and subgroups, so there is a place online for everyone, but the noise and competition for attention mean you have to choose where to be. The chapter reviews the major platforms one by one, though its numbers come from a 2016 Pew study that NYFA itself flags as already dated, so the durable value is the logic of matching a platform to your audience rather than the statistics. It closes with creating a marketing budget.

key ideas: social media as the key tool; each platform has its own culture and audience; go where your audience is; the statistics are out of date, the fit logic is not.

## NYFA, ch. 11, section "Establishing Short- and Long-Term Goals"

Added to this week in the Aug 10 reading audit; it was the one section of ch. 11 assigned nowhere, and it is the largest. It is the goal-setting frame the Outreach and Platform Strategy needs. NYFA holds that a marketing plan's most important function is a clear set of objectives, short term and long term, built on SMART goal setting (specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, timely) continued from ch. 1. The threshold question is whether you are marketing a single event or your practice in general, and NYFA starts with the practice, since an event's success rests on awareness already built. Marketing a practice has its own goals: engage the audience, stay relevant, communicate career moments, keep a dialogue going, rather than drive one specific action. On building a timeline it is unusually concrete: tie posts to outside events, to designated days and observances, to the birthday of an influence, and it flags the discomfort of linking work to a tragedy. It also names teaching and sharing the realities of an artist's life as marketing strategies that build trust, and argues for posting career updates (a residency, a panel, an article) even when you are not marketing that event, because they give context for when you do.

key ideas: SMART goals applied to marketing; marketing a practice against marketing an event; the timeline built from outside dates; teaching and transparency as marketing; updates as context for the ask.

## McCurdy, ch. 9, "Marketing and the Use of Social Media" (chapter)

Platform and plan together. McCurdy opens on the artist's dilemma (you made something and no one knows) and, citing Gail Z. Martin, argues you should build your platform and your plan at the same time. The practical section is building a fan base: start from your close circle and expand outward, and gather contact information (names, emails) at every performance, since engagement matters more than raw follower counts and a mailing list you own is worth more than a like you do not. He runs through the website as a hub, the major social platforms, and newsletters and mass mailing as the channel that keeps a relationship going.

key ideas: build platform and plan together; grow a fan base from the close circle outward; engagement over follower counts; the owned mailing list as the durable relationship channel.

## Rabideau, Toolkit, "Social Media Campaign / Video Profile / Website" (pp. 184–189)

The implementation worksheets, and the most concrete reading of the week. The Social Media Campaign how-to lays out a reusable plan: set goals (more followers, general buzz, hashtag mentions, sharing), research and choose the specific networks that reach your target audience rather than all of them, set parameters for tone, length, and timing per platform, assign a point person and a posting schedule with room for real-time engagement, and measure what works to inform the next round, all under a consistent hashtag or tagline. The Video Profile how-to treats a short video as a visual elevator pitch (clear message, practice, lighting, review with peers), and the Website/Blog how-to starts from deciding what kind of digital presence you actually need.

key ideas: a social-media campaign as a concrete plan (goals, chosen platforms, schedule, point person, measurement); the video profile as a visual pitch; the website as a deliberate choice.

## how they fit together

Kolb and McCurdy set the stance: reaching people is relational, not a broadcast, so the work is engagement and owned channels (a mailing list, a fan base built outward), not follower counts. NYFA and Rabideau handle the platforms: each has its own culture, so you go where your audience already is rather than everywhere, and NYFA's dated statistics matter less than that fit logic. Rabideau's toolkit then supplies the concrete plan, goals, chosen platforms, a schedule, a responsible person, and measurement, which is exactly where the graduate "implement" verb bites. Together they define the Outreach and Platform Strategy: the platforms and relationships you will use plus a plan specific enough to act on (outcomes 3 and 4), the point where the marketing plan built since week 6 becomes something you can enact. The reflection has students audit where their audience actually is against where they actually show up, and name the gap the strategy has to close.
