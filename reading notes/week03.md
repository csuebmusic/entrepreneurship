# week 3 reading notes

Mission & Vision. Four planning chapters that converge on mission and vision as the foundation of a plan and the filter for decisions. Summaries drawn from the chapters themselves.

## NYFA, ch. 1, "Strategic Planning for Artists" (pp. 18–33)

NYFA defines the two statements cleanly. A mission statement answers what you do and your reason for being, ideally in under two dozen words (NYFA's own: "to empower artists at critical stages in their creative lives"). A vision statement takes one of two forms: it names a future goal, or, more commonly, it lays out core values and nonnegotiables, in which case it functions as the standard every other decision is measured against, close to a values statement. Together they are a litmus test: NYFA's example is a grant that fits neither the mission nor the vision, which is worth declining even though it brings money.

For an artist, NYFA says planning begins here, with a clear sense of who you are and what you want, and it recommends writing both statements as your answer to a stranger asking what you do and why. The practical advice is to keep it simple and free of art-speak, and to test a statement verbally by describing it to friends and hearing what language sticks. The chapter then moves to an environmental scan and a SWOT analysis (strengths, including non-arts skills and resources; weaknesses, often financial or time management; opportunities; and threats, which NYFA pointedly says do not include other artists, whose work it considers unique enough to sustain its own market). Includes Exercise 1.1 (write a mission and vision) and 1.2 (SWOT).

key ideas: mission as present purpose in under two dozen words; vision as future goal or as the values-lens and nonnegotiables; mission plus vision as a litmus test; keep it short and jargon-free; the verbal test; the SWOT that follows.

## Kolb, ch. 2, "Making the dream a reality" (pp. 31–54)

Kolb frames mission, vision, and values as what to settle before writing a business plan, since they guide every later decision. He opens with the portfolio life of the artist: think and/both rather than either/or, because most creatives assemble several revenue streams (their own creative work, support work in a larger organization, and often an unrelated job). On the statements: the mission is your purpose, what you do and for whom; the vision is what you want the practice to become in the future (new products, new distribution, new markets); and values are the deep-seated beliefs that shape how the mission and vision get put into practice. He gives a clean worked example (the APAA mission, broken into who it is, what it does, for whom, and its values) and a set of sample community-theatre missions.

Two moves matter for this week. First, a creative may hold two sets of statements, one personal and one for the business, where income from the business makes the personal art possible, though the values should stay consistent across both. Second, he calls the statements a compass: they form the basis of the venture's culture, and all future decisions must align with them. Short and easily understood beats long and polished, and what matters most is that your actions reflect the statements.

key ideas: settle mission, vision, and values before planning; the portfolio career; mission as purpose (what and for whom), vision as future aspiration, values as the how; personal versus business statements; the statements as a compass; keep them short.

## Landry, ch. 1, "Plan" (pp. 1–20)

The playful, self-directed take, well suited to an independent week. Landry's frame: planning articulates something you want to change and the direction of that change, goals set the steps, dreaming supplies motivation, and vision adds emotion and value throughout. She spends real time on getting unstuck (overwhelm, self-doubt, losing track of time) and on daydreaming with purpose, using visualization and mind-wandering as where planning begins.

On the statements themselves, she works from a Unique Value Principle (UVP), what makes you and your work distinct, surfaced through a Word Cloud or a mind map, and folds it into a mission. Her mission is a short mantra or touchstone, closer to a haiku than a long poem, that states what you stand for and doubles as a filter for which clients and activities to take or decline. Worth flagging: Landry lets the terms blur, saying a mission statement is sometimes also called a vision statement when you are working on a single project, where NYFA, Kolb, and Andrews keep them distinct.

key ideas: plan, goals, dreaming, and vision as distinct roles; daydreaming with purpose; the UVP surfaced by Word Cloud or mind map; the mission as a short mantra and a filter; the blurring of mission and vision for a single project.

## Andrews, ch. 2, "Planning and Assessment" (pp. 35–66)

The most thorough planning chapter, and it lays out the whole arc: from values and goals, to vision and mission, to a strategic plan and a business plan, to SWOT, to assessment. It builds directly on Andrews's chapter 1 values exercise: the articulated values and future goals become the vision statement, which then supports crafting the mission. The vision describes where the business intends to go; the mission answers five questions, what do we do, why, how, for whom, and what benefit do we provide, combined into a direct statement a newcomer could understand.

Andrews stresses revision with feedback, and frames the mission as a guiding principle: if an opportunity or change does not align with it, seriously consider avoiding it. His example is a community arts organization that turns down support from a controversial corporation because it runs counter to a community-based mission, a concrete case of the statement doing real work. He includes a before-and-after that sharpens a vague mission (the Theatrical Theater Company) into a specific one, and ends in a SWOT analysis.

key ideas: the full planning arc; vision built from values and future goals; the five questions that build a mission; revise with feedback; the mission as a decision filter (the sponsorship example); the before/after sharpening; SWOT.

## how they fit together

All four define the same pair. A mission is present purpose: what you do, for whom, and why. A vision is future direction, or your values held up as the lens decisions pass through. All four insist the statements are useful only when they decide things: an opportunity that conflicts with them is declined, and all four insist on keeping them short and plain. Where they part is the line between the two terms: NYFA, Kolb, and Andrews keep mission and vision distinct, while Landry lets them blur for a single project. That sets up the week's two components and the reflection, which asks students to draft each statement and name where the present purpose and the future direction conflict.
