## Kolb, ch. 4, "Analyzing the product and its benefits" (pp. 82–103)

New this week. Kolb opens on commoditization: when products are undifferentiated they compete on lowest price, and his point is that creative work is the exception, distinctive by design, so it competes on communicating value rather than on price (he notes customer experience has overtaken price and quality as the main differentiator). He sorts products into convenience, comparison, and specialty categories and places most artistic work in the specialty tier, where value comes partly from exclusivity and reputation. He covers product lines and portfolios (offerings that meet the same need form a line; small ventures should keep to one or two; extensions tend to work when they speak to lifestyle and identity, "purchased for the message it sends"). The core idea for this week is the core / actual / augmented model: a product is not the object but a bundle of benefits, a core benefit (the need it fills), the actual attributes (price, quality, size), and the augmented layer around it (service, delivery, the purchase experience).

key ideas: creative work competes on value, not price; the specialty category; product as a bundle of benefits across the core, actual, and augmented levels.

## Landry, ch. 2, "Build and Test," sections "Products and Services" through "Designing and Creating Your Offering" (pp. 40–52)

Read here for the products-and-services section. Landry has you name the form your offering takes, a product, a service, or an experience, and notes that in the arts it is often a service or an experience because it happens in real time (his clowning example: a performance is a service, juggling balls are a product, a clowning party is an experience). He gives distinct sets of questions for describing each. The move that matters most for this week is his features-and-benefits table: a feature is what something is, a benefit is what it does, and benefits, the feeling, convenience, lifestyle, or identity a buyer gets, are the primary reason people buy, while features are secondary. (The "Defining Your Audiences" section of this chapter was the week 6 reading.)

key ideas: naming the form (product, service, or experience); the features-versus-benefits distinction; benefit as what the offering does for the buyer.


## how they fit together

Landry supplies the features-versus-benefits line: describe what the offering does for the buyer, not what it is. Kolb deepens it into the bundle-of-benefits model (core, actual, augmented) and reminds us that creative work sells on distinctiveness rather than price. NYFA sits earliest in the sequence, insisting you understand your own work before you frame it as an offering. Together they define the task: name what you offer and describe it as value to the audience you have profiled. That is the Product or Service Description (outcomes 2 and 4), which builds on the Audience Profile from week 6 and the Positioning Statement from week 7. The reflection has students name the several offerings a practice produces and say which one matters most to that audience.
