# week 9 reading notes

Business Models for Artists, the week that opens Module III. Four mostly-new readings on the business model, how a practice creates, delivers, and captures value on a one-page canvas. Summaries drawn from the chapters.

## NYFA, ch. 2, "The Business Model Canvas and Lean Methodology" (pp. 34–51)

The canvas itself. NYFA presents Alexander Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas, a one-page map of a venture built from nine blocks, alongside the Lean Startup method it was designed to serve. The canvas has two jobs: to organize your thinking by laying out the hypotheses that underlie every part of the business, and to push you out into the real world to turn those hypotheses into facts. NYFA leans on Steve Blank's line that no business plan survives first contact with customers, and on his finding that most start-ups fail for lack of customers rather than product problems, so the method is to fill in the canvas, then test it through customer discovery and validation and adjust. It closes with an artist-facing version, the "Artists' Business Model Canvas," and a note that "customer" can be read as patron, client, or audience member.

key ideas: the nine-block canvas as a map of hypotheses; the Lean loop of testing against real people; the canvas adapted for artists.

## Kolb, ch. 7, "Distributing your product" (pp. 151–174)

The deliver-value part of the model. Kolb treats distribution as everything involved in getting the offering from where it is made to the person who uses it, and sorts it two ways. By intensity: intensive (everywhere, which suits cheap convenience goods and rarely creative work), selective (several channels chosen for geography or segment), and exclusive (a single point of access that builds an image of exclusivity or supports a brand). By channel type: indirect, through a retailer, agent, or marketplace that handles the sale and some marketing but takes a share of the profit, versus direct, through your own studio, storefront, social media, pop-up, or craft fair, which keeps the profit and the personal relationship with the buyer.

key ideas: distribution as delivery; intensity (intensive, selective, exclusive); the direct-versus-indirect trade-off and what an intermediary takes for reach.

## Rabideau, ch. 8, "The Business Side: Laying the Groundwork for a Successful Project" (pp. 85–100)

The canvas from an artist's side, and the create-value center. Rabideau opens on mindset, critical optimism (roughly 60 percent realism, 40 percent optimism) and Peter Elbow's believing game as a way to take an idea seriously before doubting it. The core tool is the value proposition, which he gives as a fill-in template: "[Name] is a [product entity]. Through its [distinctive attributes] it provides [benefits] to [target market]." He works it through a hardware store, a dinner theater, and an individual artist, showing how precision separates a useful VP from a vague one, and stresses that the value proposition is the guiding force for every other sector of the canvas. He then walks the remaining blocks (customers and relationships, channels, partnerships) and ends on the reminder that a venture is a living thing that keeps being revised.

key ideas: the value-proposition template; the VP as the anchor the rest of the canvas serves; an artist-centered, iterative mindset.

## Landry, ch. 5, "Generate and Launch" (pp. 109–128)

The capture-value part. Read here for the revenue and business-model sections. Landry separates revenue (what people pay you, gross before costs) from profit (what remains after expenses), and defines a business model in two blunt parts: how you generate revenue (how people pay you, exactly) and what your essential costs are. He quotes Guy Kawasaki's version of the same question, who has your money in their pockets and how will you get it into yours, and advises keeping the model simple and the audience a definable niche. Revenue targets, framed as SMART goals, turn the model into something you can act on.

key ideas: revenue versus profit; the business model reduced to how you get paid and what it costs; keep it simple and specific.

## how they fit together

The four map onto one structure. NYFA supplies the canvas and the discipline behind it: a business model is a set of hypotheses you test and revise, not a fixed plan. Rabideau anchors the create-value side with the value proposition, the offer at the center that every other block serves. Kolb is the deliver-value side, the channels through which the offer reaches the audience. Landry is the capture-value side, revenue against cost, and the question of which streams actually pay. Together they define the business model as how a practice creates, delivers, and captures value, held on one page. That is the Business Model Snapshot (outcome 2), which pulls the audience, positioning, product, and value work of Module II into a working whole. The reflection has students sketch their revenue across more than one stream and locate where the model is fragile.
