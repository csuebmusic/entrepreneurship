# week 11 reading notes

Marketing, Brand & Story, the week that opens Movement IV. Four readings on the brand as the coherent story of your work, carried across contexts. Kolb 8 and Rabideau 10 are the new readings; NYFA 11 (branding) and Andrews 3 (image) are revisited for their brand-relevant sections. Summaries drawn from the chapters.

## Kolb, ch. 8, "Promoting the creative product" (pp. 175–197)

The brand chapter. Kolb treats a brand as more than a mark: it becomes an expression of the consumer's own values (buying from a venture that stands for something lets the buyer act on what they care about), so a marketing message about benefits and a brand image about values both do work. Branding also solves a trust problem in a crowded market, since people place trust in brands and look for social proof through recommendations and reviews. He stresses that a brand image matters most for intangible offerings like performance, where "the cover," the brand, has to inform the customer of the contents in advance. To build one, he suggests naming the emotional adjectives that describe your mission and asking customers what the work does for them and means to them (their memories and stories). His most useful move for this week: a brand can center benefits, but it can also center places, people, and issues, including the life story of the creative entrepreneur and the organization's mission, so long as any cause it claims is authentic.

key ideas: brand as image and values, not a logo; brand carries mission; the creator's story as a brand; clarity matters most for intangible work.

## NYFA, ch. 11, "Marketing Principles and Strategies" (pp. 167–181)

Revisited for the "Branding" section (also read in week 7). NYFA defines a brand as a customer experience carried by a collection of images and ideas (a name, logo, associations), notes that strong brands earn trust and can command a premium, and insists that the whole aim of a brand is clarity and consistency, which is precisely why many artists resist the idea that their work is a brand.

key ideas: brand as experience; clarity and consistency as the point; the artist's resistance to it.

## Rabideau, ch. 10, "Getting the Word Out: Storytelling, Marketing, and Communications Strategy" (pp. 115–132)

The story spine, by Elizabeth Hinckley. The chapter opens with "Bob's Tale," a parable in which an inventor fails with generic ads until he learns to answer three visitors from the heart: what change do you bring about (mission), what can you do that others cannot (unique value), and who are you and your company (the pitch). Those map to what she argues a stranger actually wants: why you do this (your heart and origin), what makes you special, and where you will take them. She compresses the three into one sentence she calls the Hero Declaration, a mantra and strategic direction that is the most important thing any audience should remember, and her practical claim is that building all your communications from that one line keeps your message consistent across every platform. The "story told well" section adds craft: you are the hero of your company's story and your origin story matters, and different narrators suit different media (you write the bio, a journalist writes the feature, a customer gives the endorsement).

key ideas: the three things a stranger needs (why, what makes you special, where you take them); the Hero Declaration as a one-line through-line; consistency across platforms; the origin story.

## Andrews, ch. 3, "Marketing" (pp. 67–105)

Revisited for the image-and-audience opening (the exchange, market-research, and competitive-analysis sections were the week 6 and 7 readings). Andrews frames marketing as communication aimed at outcomes, the first being awareness, and describes how a venture crafts and disseminates its image and identity through names, logos, written material, and audio or video pieces that tell the story behind a product or an artist's vision. The next step is educating the audience through information, examples, and testimonials so they can make an informed choice.

key ideas: marketing builds and spreads your image; materials that tell the story behind your vision; educating the audience toward an informed choice.

## how they fit together

Rabideau supplies the spine: under any brand is a story, and its core answers why, what makes you special, and where you take people, compressed into a single line that keeps everything consistent, which is the through-line. Kolb and NYFA define what that consistency amounts to, a brand as the image, values, and experience people attach to your work, with clarity and consistency as the whole point, and the creator's own story as fair material for it. Andrews is the reach, how marketing carries that story outward to an audience and educates them. Together they define the Brand Narrative: the through-line of your work told as a coherent story you can carry across a bio, a grant, a website, or a stage introduction without it changing shape (outcomes 3 and 4). It draws on the identity and mission of Movement I and the positioning and value of Movement II. The reflection has students tell the story of their work in the single paragraph a stranger would need, and name the thread running through it.
