# week 1 reading notes

Art, Culture & the Creative Career. Kolb 1 (8–30), Andrews 1 (1–34), Rabideau 1 (1–16), McCurdy 1. Four chapters, one from each of the required and rotation texts, read against the week's question: what does it mean to build a career as an artist. Summaries and quotations are drawn from the chapters themselves. Page numbers are the books' printed pages; McCurdy is a reflowable ebook and is cited by section heading.

## the four in one line each

- **Kolb** places art inside economic history and hands you the structural decisions: intermediary or direct, nonprofit or business, which of four legal forms, and what separates revenue from profit.
- **Andrews** maps the term from Say and Schumpeter through lean startup, then argues that arts entrepreneurship is distinctive in two ways: disruption is optional, and the exit strategy is usually the wrong goal.
- **Rabideau** reclaims the word from money toward invention, and defines the 21st-century musician by self-authorship, with portraits and a framework of five mindsets and three skill sets.
- **McCurdy** gives the labor-market reality and a personal model: performer, composer, pedagogue, intellectual, plus proactivity, goals, mentors, and a pipeline of projects.

Register: Kolb is a business textbook, Andrews an academic survey, Rabideau a manifesto, McCurdy a mentor's talk. The four disagree about method more than about premise.

## Kolb, ch. 1, "Art, culture and entrepreneurship" (pp. 8–30)

### the argument

Individual artists, creative industries, and cultural organizations have always been businesses, though many have been poor at pricing, marketing, and distribution (p. 8). Art has always been priced, distributed, and promoted; historically the promoter, agent, or gallery did it rather than the creator. Communication technology now allows the creator to do it directly. The stated mission of the book is that business skill buys back time: fewer hours on a second job for the individual, fewer hours on fundraising inside a nonprofit (p. 8).

### the historical account (pp. 9–11)

Kolb builds the chapter's frame from a history of how art has been valued.

- The definition of art as an object of individual expression with no utilitarian function is accepted only from the eighteenth century (Staniszewski 1995). Before that, art objects and performances were an expression of a society's culture and were expected to meet needs, pragmatic (pottery for eating) or spiritual (statues and music for worship) (pp. 9–10).
- "Technical skill, not vision, was considered necessary to produce art" (p. 10, citing Greffe 2002). The Renaissance elevated art above mechanical skill but still treated it as learnable. Vision and genius join technical skill only in the eighteenth century.
- Scarcity produces value alongside aesthetics (Budd 1995). Middle Ages: courts and churches. Eighteenth-century market economy: merchants, who bought both for beauty and for resale.
- The art/craft division: art as the expression of the artist's inner vision, craft as made for someone else's appreciation. Kolb calls the division artificial, since craft can also come from inner vision.
- Highbrow and lowbrow: Kolb dates the split to the seventeenth century (citing Woodmansee 1994) and reads it as a class judgment. As more people could afford art they chose romantic novels and music hall over high art; the upper classes read that as a deficit of intelligence, hence "dumbing down" (p. 11, with Gans 1997). The formulation to put in front of students: "If the public is not willing to pay for art, that is the fault of the public and not the art. Under this view, art that is popular with the public cannot be good."
- The counterexample: Renaissance artists in Italy were savvy businessmen, Shakespeare's plays were produced for ticket profit, and theatrical companies "wrote contracts, demanded fees that they thought were commensurate with their talent and would walk away if the commission was not satisfactory" (p. 11, citing Levine 1988).

**The load-bearing claim:** "Art, therefore, has two values: Its aesthetic value and its monetary value" (p. 11), and the two can combine in one buyer's motivation. A purchase can be for beauty, for resale, or for both.

**Contestable, and worth contesting aloud.** Kolb's period dates do not line up cleanly with her sources: Woodmansee's subject is the eighteenth century, and Levine's *Highbrow/Lowbrow* is about nineteenth-century America, so "developed in the seventeenth century" is Kolb's compression rather than the cited scholarship. The claim that only in the eighteenth century did vision matter is also a strong reading. Both are usable as a live question rather than a fact: whose interest does a periodization of "when art became autonomous" serve.

### the business of art (pp. 13–15)

- Two markets: **consumer market** (individuals, personal use, gifts, display at home; marketing stresses personal benefit) and **business market** (schools, civic organizations, corporations, collections; marketing stresses quality and price).
- **Intermediary:** the party between creator and purchaser, a gallery, dealer, agent, or promoter, who has done the research on consumer needs and frees the artist to work. Intermediaries promote and take part of the profit, and generally only take on artists with an established market (Hadley 2021).
- The chapter's most quotable line: "Entrepreneurship can be considered as just another means of creative expression" (p. 13).
- Artists can now act as their own intermediaries: online studios, social media, payment technology that works at distance. The cost is visibility among everything else online, and the demand that you also inform and entertain, not only sell. Kolb's specific advice: use social media to educate the public about your field with stories and videos of how the work is made, and to share information about other creatives and upcoming events even when not immediately relevant to what you are selling.
- The performing-arts version of the choice: an agent books venues for a percentage and gets paid whether or not the house fills, so agents prefer names. Self-booking means contracting the venue, taking the risk of unsold tickets, and keeping the upside.
- On money: "For both the creative and the entrepreneur, financial success is not the ultimate goal, but rather money is a means to an end." For the creative it means the work is appreciated, and it "is a means to pay the bills, buy more materials and have the freedom to be more creative" (p. 15).
- Kolb also notes the pressure behind all this: the decline of government grant funding pushes creatives toward earned revenue (p. 15).

### the structural decisions (pp. 15–19)

**Three organizational forms**, differing by ownership, taxation, and purpose:

- **Government:** formed by elected officials; core functions and public facilities including theatres and museums; paid from taxes.
- **Nonprofit** (also NGO, charity): formed by citizens to meet a public need; may charge fees but cannot cover expenses from them, so relies on grants and contributions; usually tax exempt. Kolb's practical note: donors prefer entrepreneurial nonprofits, on the reasoning that they are likelier to meet their mission.
- **Business:** formed to provide a product for money, covering production, overhead, and profit.

**Four business structures** (Table 1.1, p. 17), traded off by risk and reward:

- **Sole proprietorship:** one owner, all risk, all reward, no shared decisions. Most creative startups. Appeals to founders unwilling to compromise on product or mission.
- **Partnership:** two or more owners, two sources of funds and skills, risk of disagreement. Each partner can be liable for the other's debts. Failures are usually personal: differing management styles, habits, commitment. Kolb's remedy is clear goals, regular meetings, and a written legal agreement.
- **Corporation:** the company has the legal standing of a person, so revenue goes to the corporation and personal assets are shielded, which is what limited liability means. Established in Britain in the 1840s partly on the argument that limited liability "would enable poor people to start businesses because they would not have to put all their money at risk" (p. 18, citing Micklethwait and Wooldridge 2003).
- **Cooperative:** producers share expenses such as a store lease and marketing; revenue is tracked separately per owner. Lower operating cost, no control over what else is sold beside your work.
- **Nonprofit** as a structure: no owners, only managers, plus a board of directors with ultimate responsibility. Excess is reinvested in the mission rather than distributed.

### the money vocabulary (pp. 20–21)

Define these in seminar; they recur in weeks 9, 10, and 14.

- **Revenue:** money received from sales. Not profit.
- **Gross profit:** revenue minus the cost of producing the product.
- **Overhead:** all the costs of operating the business.
- **Net profit:** gross profit minus overhead. Only positive net profit supports the owner's living expenses.
- **Fixed costs:** due whether or not anything sells (rent, utilities).
- **Variable costs:** per item produced (materials, wages for its production).
- **Capital:** startup money sufficient to cover costs until the business sustains itself.
- **Break-even:** covering all expenses with revenue. Profit begins after.

Kolb names confusing gross with net as a common cause of business failure, and notes that nonprofits can cover the gap with grants and donations while for-profits cannot.

### paths, motives, and the reversal (pp. 21–28)

- **Three ways to launch:** buy an existing business (money and skill; advice is to work in a similar business at least six months first), start a full-time business with a leased location (fastest need for revenue, hardest to hold a day job), or run a **lifestyle business** (an existing interest turned into revenue, often run from where the owner lives, sometimes never needing retail space).
- **Supplemental income:** incomes fluctuate with commissions and sales, so a second occupation may be necessary. Kolb's framing: the more revenue you generate from your own work, the less you work for someone else's paycheck.
- **Three strategic goals by type:** small business, to provide a living for owner and family; traditional entrepreneur, growth toward competing with established firms; creative entrepreneur, enough profit to support a creative lifestyle.
- **Term history:** "entrepreneurial" becomes common only in the 1980s. Post-war US dominance made employment the rational choice through the 1950s and 1960s; the 1970s brought international competition and microprocessors; social media in the twenty-first century advantaged small firms that could promote and sell directly.
- **Ending a venture** is not failure: taste changes, or life does, and the same person may start another later (Power 2018).
- **Business plan:** "simply the dream of the creative individual put into writing." Long plans of 30 to 40 pages are for outside funders; a startup can write a short one as a road map, and should feel free to deviate and experiment (Yaghcha 2023). Components: description of business (including organizational structure and the mission, vision, and value statements), product (benefits and competitive advantage, not just output), customer (demographic, geographic, psychographic), distribution, promotion, financial projections.

**The reversal at p. 28, which is the sharpest teaching moment in the chapter.** The traditional entrepreneur begins with a consumer need and changes the product if research finds no market. The creative entrepreneur begins with the product they wish to create, then analyzes it for benefits in order to find the customer segment most likely to buy. Same components, opposite order. This reversal is the premise of the entire portfolio sequence: identity and values first, audience and positioning after.

Box 1.3 (p. 22, de Wal 2024) lists five attributes of successful artists: art at the core of life, understanding how art works in the business world, strong work ethic balancing making and marketing, resilience, and spending time only with supportive people.

**Key terms introduced:** intermediary, consumer market, business market, sole proprietorship, partnership, corporation, cooperative, nonprofit, revenue, gross profit, net profit, overhead, fixed and variable cost, capital, break-even, lifestyle business, business plan.

## Andrews, ch. 1, "What Is Arts Entrepreneurship?" (pp. 1–34)

The longest and most systematic of the four, and the one that gives the course its vocabulary. It runs from definitions to launch mechanics to the arts-specific case, then out to social entrepreneurship, education, community, and succession.

### definitions (pp. 1–2)

- **Merriam-Webster:** an entrepreneur is someone who organizes, manages, and assumes the risks of a business or enterprise.
- **Drucker**, quoted at length: entrepreneurship "sees change as normal and indeed as healthy," and sees the major task "as doing something different rather than doing better what is already being done."
- **Say** coined the term, and Drucker reads the coinage as "a manifesto and as a declaration of dissent: the entrepreneur upsets and disorganizes."
- **Schumpeter:** "creative destruction."
- Disruption cases: Google, Facebook, Amazon; Netflix against videotape rental; Uber and Lyft against taxis; Airbnb against hotels; Amazon against brick and mortar.

### the pivotal claim (pp. 2–3)

Disruption is not required. Andrews gives three counterexamples: a photographer teaching digital technique to young learners nearby, an online business selling fine art by artists working on political and social issues, a composer-performer supplying original music for commercial applications. The arts entrepreneur still organizes, manages, and assumes risk, and additionally must "work toward connecting their own values, passions, and long-term commitments to their creative practice with measurable consumer interest."

"Art entrepreneurs have the freedom to decide what their new venture is based on, where it is located, and whom it is for. They also have the freedom to choose what type of entrepreneurship they want to pursue, either through disruption and 'creative destruction' or through the introduction of a new business that finds a home in an existing marketplace alongside, and even in collaboration with, similar organizations" (pp. 2–3).

### where a venture starts: values (pp. 3–5)

Two starting points. One is prior employment: a film student who interned at a distributor designing a venture from what they saw. Andrews recommends employment, internship, or volunteering in the field before founding, and returns to this at pp. 26–27.

The other is the **Center for Cultural Innovation's *Business of Art* workbook**, three exercises in sequence:

1. Explore personal and professional values. Andrews's worked examples: community building, creative inspiration, and opportunity generation produce a neighborhood teaching venture; personal expression, international relationships, and cultural exchange produce a venture moving cultural products across borders.
2. Imagine your future life in detail, professional and personal. This is the vision exercise, and it produces written goals.
3. Combine the two into a brief, clearly composed **vision statement**, revised as you progress, that guides the decisions of both business and life.

Then **Guillebeau's** three lessons from *The $100 Startup*:

- **Convergence:** the intersection between your passion and what others care about.
- **Skill transformation:** turning talents into a business concept, including skills in communication, accounting, and managing people.
- **Passion or skill plus usefulness equals success:** the founder's abilities combined with the customer's perception that the offering improves their life.

Guillebeau also supplies the discipline of building what the customer actually wants rather than what the founder thinks they should want. Andrews's example: a founder wants an electronic dance nightclub; the surrounding region wants an intimate room for world music, jazz, and acoustic singer-songwriters.

### method: lean startup (pp. 5–8, 12–13)

- **Minimum viable product (MVP):** a no-frills prototype, flaws included, built quickly to see whether it captures interest. For an arts venture it is a small-scale version of the eventual thing: an arts education business starting with a handful of students to test content and measure response.
- **Build-measure-learn:** prototype rapidly, test with customers, learn what works.
- **Validated learning:** empirically demonstrating that the venture has discovered basic truths about its customers and delivers value to them.
- Testing methods for artists: focus groups, surveys, interviews; a visual artist inviting potential buyers to comment on distinctiveness, pricing, availability, exclusivity; performers presenting works in progress, in pop-up or invitation-only settings.
- Andrews is candid that the lean method's treatment of marketing, aesthetics, and even product finalization as extraneous expense "might seem extreme," and offers it as suited to a product that needs many iterations before it connects.

### three risks (pp. 6–7)

- **Financial:** money in and out; obligations to investors, lenders, crowdfunding backers, suppliers, landlords, employees; bills paid on time and credit maintained.
- **Reputational:** launching creates an identity for the venture and for the people in it. False advertising, low quality, or dishonesty gets replaced by a competitor with a better reputation.
- **Legal:** tax filings, insurance, corporate reporting, workplace safety, employee relations, licensing, intellectual property, and exposure to suits in both directions.

### customers and the adoption curve (pp. 9–11)

- **Benefits over features.** Andrews's worked pair: "a unique opportunity to experience dance from a rarely presented culture" (benefit) against "an evening of music and dance" (feature). He concedes that some products resist this, and suggests studying how galleries and museums write their exhibit copy, which usually tells the story of an artist or a form.
- **Adoption categories** (Kawasaki's *The Art of the Start*, drawing on Rogers and Shoemaker): **innovators** (seek novelty and risk, want it first), **early adopters** (sense intuitively that it serves their needs, unafraid of the untested), **early majority** (follow validation by experts, reviews, peers), **late majority** (wait for mass acceptance), **laggards** (buy only when there is no choice).
- The practical instruction: comb your existing network for innovators and early adopters, use their feedback for radical changes if warranted, and treat critics and media figures in that group as future advocates.

### growth, measurement, pivots (pp. 11–15)

- Attracting too many people too early risks disappointing them: a crowdfunding campaign aiming for 100 backers and finding 1,000 may fail on fulfillment.
- **Scale is a choice.** "Even in the case of an arts venture that only strives to attract customers in the dozens or hundreds, there needs to be advance planning." And later: "The business founder has the freedom to decide which measurement of success works for their intentions."
- **Four ingredients** for a product that reaches a growing audience: a unique offering genuinely connected to a target base; awareness raised through marketing; funds raised and a collaborative team hired; a set of key values and principles adhered to.
- **Pivot:** a significant change in product, marketing, or direction that serves the same goals. "A pivot is not an abandonment of the company's overall values, goals, and objectives, it is a redirection of the organization's process for accomplishing those aims." The basketball analogy is his: one foot planted, ninety degrees, pass instead of drive.

### the arts-specific case (pp. 15–19)

**Open-ended ownership against the exit strategy** (pp. 16–17). Conventional startups often build the ending in from the beginning. **Harvest** is the phase where founders and financial partners take their return, by going public or being acquired. Other exits: merging with a competitor, or dissolving and moving on. **Serial entrepreneurs** repeat the cycle. The arts entrepreneur may choose this, "however, for the arts entrepreneur who is building a business based on their lifetime interest in a creative practice ... the idea of an exit strategy is the opposite of what they may have in mind." Roberts and Green's three motivations, quoted at p. 17: deep and abiding passion for the art form, a desire to spend a career making a living involved with it, and a commitment to being the means by which the artist's work reaches the public, which entails being in it for the long haul.

**Social entrepreneurship** (pp. 17–19). The difference from traditional entrepreneurship is the intended mission. Four configurations Andrews names:

- for-profit, commercial success first, social impact second;
- for-profit, social benefits first, financial reward second;
- for-profit, both in equal measure;
- for-profit forming with the intent to acquire nonprofit status over time, using a fiscal sponsor for contributed income in the meantime.

Two corporate forms: the **B-Corporation** or Benefit Corporation, and the **L3C** (limited liability low profit company), available in certain states. Social ventures have all the same risks and compliance obligations as any other. His examples: a community-based theater in a disadvantaged neighborhood involving residents as playwrights, actors, and production workers; an arts education organization teaching creative skills to people in psychological or emotional difficulty; a gallery on environmental themes; a performing space pairing political activism with art forms.

**Nonprofit** (p. 22): "the term nonprofit does not mean that the owners or employees of the organization will not receive appropriate compensation for their efforts." Many pay well and provide benefits. What is unique is that excess income is not distributed; it is reinvested or reserved. Andrews names race, gender, aging, immigration, economic disparity, and climate change as guiding concepts on which arts ventures have been built.

### the rest of the chapter, in brief (pp. 19–31)

- **Education** as mission, as complementary program, or as market. Programs can attract foundation, corporate, and government funding, reachable through a fiscal sponsor without nonprofit status.
- **Fine art as a business model** (pp. 21–22): access to materials and skills, quality control as you scale, dependence on suppliers whose quality becomes yours, and the fact that fine art represents the artist's own vision rather than a concept formulated by someone else.
- **Experiences as product** (pp. 22–23): specific to time, place, and context; the design priority is the customer's intellectual and emotional engagement.
- **Digital products** (p. 23): a crowded marketplace where the question is what makes a free user convert, whether convenience (the Pandora model) or access to specialized material.
- **Venues** (pp. 23–24): renting temporarily to test audience response is the MVP form for a presenting organization, the same move a restaurant makes with a pop-up.
- **Stakeholders** (pp. 24–26), each attracted differently: **partners** (join at founder level, assume risk), **supporters** (media contacts, board members, pro bono advisors, volunteers), **patrons** or customers, **suppliers** (their reliability becomes your brand), **investors** (money and influence, usually shared control), **donors** (no financial return expected, but focused on mission performance).
- **Online platforms** (pp. 27–28) for fundraising (Kickstarter, Indiegogo, GoFundMe), marketing, and community building. His caution: communicating exclusively about opportunities to buy drives people away. Not all relationships need be transactional; an unconverted reader may advocate on your behalf and produce more effect than a single sale.
- **Collaboration** (pp. 28–29): in the arts and culture sector, like-minded organizations frequently become allies rather than competition. Galleries in a district marketing jointly through art walks; a photographer or designer referring a client to someone better suited or free.
- **Launch, growth, succession** (pp. 30–31), with the note that corporate form matters if the intent is to leave a thriving business behind.

**Key terms introduced:** creative destruction, disruption, minimum viable product, build-measure-learn, validated learning, pivot, harvest, serial entrepreneur, exit strategy, innovators and early adopters through laggards, benefits against features, social entrepreneurship, B-Corporation, L3C, fiscal sponsor, financial, reputational, and legal risk.

## Rabideau, ch. 1, "Being a 21st-Century Musician" (pp. 1–16)

The music-native chapter and the frame for the book. Its rhetorical mode is advocacy, and it should be read that way in seminar.

### the reclamation (pp. 1–3)

Rabideau wrote "Entrepreneurship and the Artist-Revolutionary" in 2015 for two reasons: to celebrate innovative musicians, and "to reclaim the word entrepreneurship. I wanted to move the focus away from monetary gain and make it more about the act of invention."

He prints an objection he received, from Michael Drapkin: "Seriously? Artists, and in particular musicians, are one of the most conservative groups I have ever met. They seek to distinguish themselves by being better at 'sameness' than the other guy, and live in fear of being truly different or innovative. It sounds good, but as they say in Texas, 'That dawg don't hunt.'" Rabideau concedes the comment "still resonate[s] with me on a deep level" and then relocates the fault: not with musicians but with "the schools of music and conservatories across the nation that have placed creativity off to the side in favor of exclusively training excellence."

His claim of kinship: artists and entrepreneurs are curious, creative, collaborative, tenacious. Entrepreneurs begin with **problem-finding**, identifying the issues and opportunities they are best equipped to address, "driven by the question 'What can be?' rather than 'What is?'" The shared capacity is "the ability to address complexity and thrive while playing in the messy, fertile space of uncertainty, ambiguity, and promise" (pp. 2–3).

### the institutional and impact evidence (pp. 3–4)

- Programs: Eastman's Arts Leadership Program, University of South Carolina's Spark Laboratory, DePauw's 21st-Century Musician initiative; fresh inc, created by Fifth House Ensemble's Melissa Snoza, "equal parts new music and new ideas."
- **Decoda**, out of the ACJW fellowship program, whose Performance + Community work engages the homeless and hospitalized, the incarcerated, and passersby through Street Studio workshops.
- **Musicambia**, founded by Decoda violist Nathan Schram, developing conservatories inside prisons. Schram's question, worth reading aloud: "How many of you would say that music has been life changing? And if we believe this to be true, then who more than those incarcerated in our prison systems are poised to take advantage of an opportunity to engage in the life-changing experience of making music?"
- The scale correction: taking on Musicambia's scope would be "a fundamental misunderstanding of the very essence and strength of the entrepreneurial mindset, the ability to look at a local problem, where many of these opportunities reside, and invent a unique solution that draws upon one's skills, knowledge, passions, and resources."
- The diagnosis: musicians have "fallen victim to a narrowly defined set of professional standards focused on memorizing and mastering set repertoire and a list of career options that hasn't expanded much since the Middle Ages, particularly if you are a classical musician."
- The alternative, and the phrase that recurs across the book: "a life of means, the ability to provide for those whom they love most; a life of meaning, doing good work and making an impact within their community; and a chance to give back."
- Schram again, on the moment: "for me, we are living in the second golden age of music where anything is possible." Rabideau's gloss: musicians of the first golden age "were composers, performers, improvisers, and connectors. In a word: they were creatives."
- **Yo-Yo Ma** as the model artist-revolutionary: he could have kept playing the Bach suites and done fine; instead, Silk Road Ensemble, the Goat Rodeo Sessions, an appearance with Misty Copeland, Silk Road's Global Musician Workshop, and the Citizen Musician Initiative with the Chicago Symphony. Also named: Gustavo Dudamel, Peter Seymour of Project Trio, Jayme Stone of the Lomax Project.

### Sandow, "What Is a 21st-Century Musician?" (pp. 5–9)

Greg Sandow's guest section defines the type by three habits:

- They go their own way.
- They perform or compose the music they like, in the way they like, and that music is not always classical.
- They make their own opportunities, shaping careers like entrepreneurs and often producing their own performances.

The contrast with the old rules is the useful part: if you played clarinet, you played the clarinet repertoire; your musical life was defined by that music; "your role was to serve the composers who wrote it, and your own identity mattered much less." You did not control your performances, whoever booked you decided how it looked and what the program notes said, and "your audience would be their audience. You wouldn't have one of your own."

Five portraits, each with a specific mechanism worth naming rather than paraphrasing:

- **Anderson & Roe**, duo pianists: they arrange anything they want to play, from a Michael Jackson song to the first-act finale of *Così fan tutte*, present it with strong visual production in music videos, and now sell scores of their transcriptions.
- **Stewart Goodyear**, pianist: the complete Beethoven sonatas in a single-day marathon, for which he trained like an athlete, now repeated annually at major venues. He composes, and he writes his own liner notes, "these are his performances played his way."
- **James Ross**, conductor: performances of *Prelude to the Afternoon of a Faun* and *Appalachian Spring* played from memory and danced while played; a *Petrushka* with the musicians doubling as actors, later taken up by the New York Philharmonic; students given authority over concert dress; at the National Orchestral Institute, musicians given the power to produce their own concerts.
- **Mason Bates**, composer and dance DJ at once, chosen by Muti with Anna Clyne as Chicago Symphony composer in residence, and responsible with Clyne for turning MusicNOW into a series drawing large young crowds.
- **Lara Downes**, pianist: an alt-classical series, the Artist Sessions, hosted in clubs and spaces for people who never attend classical events; individual releases including *13 Ways of Looking at the Goldberg* and *Exiles' Café*, promoted with video trailers.

Rabideau's caveat before the portraits: entrepreneurship "is not a shortcut for the 10,000 practice hours"; every artist in the book is at the top of their game technically and artistically.

### Doser, "Artistry at the Core" (pp. 10–11)

James Doser, director of Eastman's Institute for Music Leadership. This is the chapter's clearest statement of the course's impact and equity threads.

- "Our mission is to add value to the lives of others through artistry."
- Artistry is characterized by honesty, integrity, and the intent to move people in profound ways.
- "Artistry is present in all cultures, genders, ages, races, and economic strata."
- Artistry may include, but is not defined by, technical mastery, advanced degrees, or critical and popular acclaim.
- The line that draws the boundary: "If we remove artistry from the foundation of our entrepreneurial projects, then let's not call ourselves artists who are entrepreneurial thinkers, but rather entrepreneurs who use musical tools in our work."
- And the concession: there is nothing wrong with using artistry to build a rewarding career and a stable income.

Rabideau follows with the composer analogy: see the big picture first, scope, instrumentation, moments of arrival, transitions. No composer starts at measure one and never lifts the pen. "So what if you were to think of your life's work as your masterpiece? What do the big moments look like? ... Who will be in your ensemble? And who will be your audience?" Then: "The revolutionary artist takes authority over his artistry and path."

### the framework (pp. 12–13)

**Five mindsets:**

- **Unquenchable curiosity.** Focusing outside ourselves to find intersections between the issues that matter to us, our art form, and meaningful change.
- **Purposeful creativity.** Thinking like an inventor who identifies the problems he is best equipped to address.
- **Strategic collaboration.** Identifying the "strengths in the room" and leveraging them.
- **Passionate persistence.** Tenacity and grit learned in the practice room as the greatest transferable asset.
- **Critical optimism.** Peter Elbow's **believing game** rather than the doubting game: optimistic in seeing yourself as an agent of change, critical when taking feasible action.

**Three skill sets:** telling idea-driven stories; funding priceless ideas (earned revenue, donor relations, crowdfunding, grant writing); moving ideas to action, where assessment is "generating projects that advance the art form, connect with new audiences, and strengthen existing ones," including "impacting those at the margins."

The framework compresses into **four keys**: curiosity, creativity, collaboration, tenacity. On the last, David Taylor's phrase, "embracing the joy of the struggle."

### crossroads and closing (pp. 13–15)

Rabideau's own path is told plainly: he wanted the Chicago Symphony, studied with Frank Crisafulli, auditioned for Crisafulli's chair when he retired, and lost to "some young guy with an Australian accent." Then "professional autopilot": master's, sabbatical replacement, doctorate, professorship, tenure. Then a postdoc in a Center for Creativity, a nonprofit arts organization, a live jazz radio show from New York, a ballet, and, "most radical of all, I started asking questions." His question to hold over the seminar: "What if, in the real-life game of musical chairs, there is a seat for everyone?"

David Cutler, quoted at pp. 14–15: "There are people who see one or two opportunities, or zero. And then there are people who see 1,000 opportunities. I have never met someone who sees five opportunities." Rabideau's use of it: the challenge is **divergent thinking** about possibilities "amid the sea of sameness currently flooding our profession," then **convergent thinking** to hone in on the ideas that best advance the work.

**Key terms introduced:** problem-finding, artist-revolutionary, citizen-musician, a life of means and a life of meaning, the five mindsets, the three skill sets, the believing game, divergent and convergent thinking.

## McCurdy, Goodstein & Lapin, ch. 1, "A Model for Success"

Epigraph, Herbie Hancock: "Art is what you do, not who you are." Worth holding against the week's component, which asks students to write who they are as an artist. The tension is productive rather than accidental.

### defining yourself as an artist

Artists "view the world through a different lens," and "our task is to find the beauty, the ugly, the tragedy, and the triumphs in life." Art can reflect love and anger, and also the darker side of human experience, "such as war or the genocide of a particular population or ethnic group." The summary line: "art is a reflection of and interpretation of life."

The **seagull syndrome**: seagulls at the beach are never just hanging out, they are always working. "There are no days off! This is the life of an artist." Lapin's marginal correction is part of the text: Michelangelo forgot to eat, and "while focus and concentration is important, we have to remember to take care of ourselves."

### the labor market, stated plainly

The account here is blunt.

- Over twenty-five years: "the days of touring big bands, steady orchestral jobs, or full-time teaching positions are largely over." Recording studio work is nearly nonexistent, most live, film, and television music "comes out of a box," touring Broadway gigs are rare and largely replaced by reduced orchestras dominated by rhythm players and synthesizers. The same diminishment applies to actors, designers, theater and dance professionals; for visual artists, selling one work at a time is a daunting prospect.
- Streaming has expanded outlets in television and digital media, but "arts education within higher education has not kept pace with current job opportunities."
- The supply side: a 2015 College Music Society report found the number of baccalaureate degrees awarded in the visual and performing arts increased by 123 percent between 1991 and 2011.
- The conclusion: "the art professions have a supply and demand problem," and the norm is short-term contracts, commercial opportunities, and living project to project.
- The pipeline account: students enter through school ensembles and classes for fun and community, are identified early, recruited with tuition discounts, and arrive at the end of four years having developed the talent with "no idea how to transition their artistic training and four-year degree into a sustainable career as an artist."

### "fear-based education"

Jazz bassist and educator **John Clayton's** term for defaulting into graduate school out of fear rather than a clear reason, and then repeating the move from master's to doctorate. The authors' position: not against advanced degrees, but "there should be a clear understanding for why an advanced degree is being pursued," and graduate school "should only be a last resort when no other options are available or when it specifically addresses your career goals." They name the economics: return on investment, cost, multi-year delay in entering the workforce, long-term debt, and the way assistantships and talent-based scholarships perpetuate a cycle of too many qualified graduates for too few jobs.

Then all three authors put their own histories on the page: McCurdy went straight through three degrees with no idea what shape his career would take and calls himself lucky; Lapin worked at the family ice cream shop, talked his way into a symphony internship, and went back to graduate school "with very little plan for my artistic future"; Goodstein wanted principal clarinet in the New York Philharmonic, discovered in his first weeks as an undergraduate that he sat in the middle of the section, and switched to music education as "nothing but a safety net at the time."

**A live question for a graduate seminar.** This is a chapter arguing against reflexive graduate study, assigned to graduate students in week one. Put it to them directly: ask what their reason is, and have them answer it in writing. It sets up the Creative Identity Statement and it is the honest version of the reading.

### the twenty-first-century artist

Justin DiCioccio's three traits, plus one the authors add:

- **Performer:** requisite skills of the highest caliber, hours invested daily. "The perfection of technique allows the artist to 'speak' with expression and clarity."
- **Composer:** every artist should engage in some form of composition, broadly construed (score, choreography, novel, script). Voice is discovered through making.
- **Pedagogue:** every artist will teach at some point, whether educating audiences through the work or working as an arts educator. McCurdy's cautionary anecdote: a major artist opening a masterclass before 500 people with "Does anybody have any questions?"
- **Intellectual**, the authors' addition: a citizen of the planet and a critical thinker, reading widely, attending plays, visiting museums and libraries, tracking current events and history in order to navigate "24/7 news, extraordinary political and cultural polarization, and a devaluing of the traditional arts industries." The stated payoff is depth and breadth in the work: "successful artists are curious about many topics."

### what is required

Drawn from Covey's *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*:

- **Be proactive.** "As an artist, you must become an initiator, a creator." Engage in activities that lead others to present or commission the work. Envision the end of the creation at the beginning: what the finished work looks like, in what venue, with whom.
- **Leadership against management.** Leadership as the right-brain, philosophical activity; management as organizing the schedule for creating, touring, rehearsals, and business.
- **Win-win**, particularly in ensembles and collaborations. The hiring reality named directly: a group leader vetting talent asks whether the person plays well with others, shows up on time, and takes care of business.
- **Stick and stay.** Determination through negative reviews, low attendance, and no bookings. Examples: Snarky Puppy playing empty rooms while Michael League maxed out credit cards, now at major festivals with four Grammys; J. K. Rowling rejected by twelve publishers; Misty Copeland told repeatedly she did not have the right body size and shape before becoming principal at American Ballet Theatre; Fei Fei still practicing eight hours a day. "As an artist, you will be told 'no' far more times than you will hear 'yes.'"

### it's not about the money, yet

The premise is vocational: "One becomes an artist because that 'little voice' inside reminds you that this is what you were born to do. There is no Plan B." Social media and talent shows present a skewed version of the commitment required; the van-to-Lear-jet story of Richard Carpenter's band is the exception that proves it. The formative years go to honing skills with an eye toward being paid.

**Mentorship** is named as "perhaps one of the most important aspects of this development": someone with real success who will tell you the truth, and whose most productive conversations are often about life rather than craft. McCurdy's thirty-year relationship with David Baker is the extended case, ending on "no you, no me." Kenny Barron on Dizzy Gillespie: mentorship as observation, "a lot of non-musical stuff of how he treated people."

The counterweight to audience-chasing: "Crowd approval should not be the motivation for creating your art. Imagine if Picasso, Beethoven, Duke Ellington, Alvin Ailey, or Miles Davis had created their art with the intention of trying to please the public."

### what is an artist entrepreneur

"By definition, an entrepreneur is someone who engages in the art of innovation and risk-taking for profit in business." The operative word is risk-taking, and the authors locate the risk in vulnerability: "Each time an artist steps on stage, paints a landscape, writes a script, or creates a film, they are taking a risk. As artists, we allow ourselves to become vulnerable by sharing our stories and a portion of ourselves with the public. The same kind of vulnerability we experience as an artist, we also face as we create and launch our projects." Artists should take personal control of their careers, and even those with management or an agent need more than cursory knowledge of how the work is monetized.

### self-evaluation and goals

The self-evaluation questions, usable verbatim as a seminar prompt: Do you initiate artistic opportunities or wait to be called? Are you part of the local artistic community? Do you attend events presented by colleagues and peers? Do you constantly evaluate the quality of your own work? Do you ask for constructive feedback? Are you an effective self-critic, and do you listen to others whose opinion you value?

**The project pipeline.** Apple has three or four iPhone generations in development while releasing one; Disney is planning the next expansion while building the current one. "As you launch one of your projects, there should be at least three or four other projects that are at various stages of production." Marina Lomazov's version: "It's like a chess game. You set up for the future. You need to be at least two or three moves ahead."

**Goals:** short-term (three to six months) and long-term (five and ten years), written, printed, and posted where they are visible, treated as a work in process, and each paired with the steps that will achieve it. The worked example: to dance with American Ballet Theatre, attend live performances, meet the artistic director, ask to meet the person who would one day hire you, which is "an audition of sorts." Networks and mentors are the accelerant. Luck, in Lomazov's frame, is "when opportunity and preparation collide."

**Key terms introduced:** seagull syndrome, fear-based education, the four traits (performer, composer, pedagogue, intellectual), be proactive, stick and stay, artist entrepreneur, the project pipeline, short-term and long-term goals.

## where the four converge

- **Entrepreneurship is not primarily about money.** Kolb: money is a means to pay bills, buy materials, and buy freedom. Rabideau: move the focus from monetary gain to the act of invention. Andrews: the founder's values and long-term commitment come before the market. McCurdy: it's not about the money, yet.
- **The artist can now be the intermediary.** Kolb argues it from technology and history; Sandow argues it from control ("your audience would be their audience. You wouldn't have one of your own"); Rabideau's portraits are all instances of it; McCurdy's proactivity is the disposition version.
- **The venture starts from the founder rather than from the market.** Andrews's values-vision-statement sequence, Kolb's reversal at p. 28, Rabideau's "what impact do you hope to make," and McCurdy's "defining yourself as an artist" all locate the first move inside the artist. This is the premise of the portfolio's ordering.
- **Artistry belongs to everyone.** Doser: artistry "is present in all cultures, genders, ages, races, and economic strata." Andrews names race, gender, aging, immigration, economic disparity, and climate change as legitimate foundations for a venture, and Musicambia and the community-based theater are the worked cases. This is the first appearance of the power and identity thread that weeks 5 and 15 develop.
- **Scale is chosen, not given.** Andrews states it outright; Kolb's three strategic goals encode it; Rabideau's local-problem correction is the same point in a different register.

## where they conflict

Four fault lines to run the seminar on. Each is real in the texts rather than manufactured.

1. **Is the artist already an entrepreneur, or does the artist have to become one?** Rabideau says the two are "remarkably similar" by disposition, and Kolb agrees that creativity and business are "a natural fit," with Box 1.3 arguing the traits overlap. Drapkin's objection, printed inside Rabideau's own chapter, says musicians are among the most conservative people he has met, competing at sameness. Rabideau does not refute it, he relocates the blame onto conservatory training. Ask the seminar whose account of their own training is more accurate.

2. **Does the venture start from the work or from the audience?** Kolb's creative entrepreneur starts with the product they want to make and then hunts for the segment. Andrews's lean method starts with an MVP and lets validated learning reshape the product, and Guillebeau insists on what the customer actually wants rather than what the founder thinks they should have. McCurdy forbids making for crowd approval outright. These are not compatible in practice, and week 6 through week 8 will make students choose.

3. **What counts as artistry.** Doser draws a hard line: remove artistry from the foundation and you are "entrepreneurs who use musical tools." McCurdy's epigraph goes the other way: "Art is what you do, not who you are." Kolb's aesthetic-versus-monetary distinction sits between them, and his highbrow-lowbrow history suggests that the line between art and product is itself a class artifact.

4. **Optimism against the numbers.** Rabideau's second golden age, "anything is possible, everything is possible," and McCurdy's 123 percent degree growth against thinned traditional employment are descriptions of the same field. Both may be true; ask what would have to be the case for both to be true at once, and what that implies about where a career actually gets built.

A fifth, smaller, and useful for graduate students specifically: **the exit strategy question.** Andrews frames open-ended commitment as the arts-native default. Kolb, at p. 26, says ending a venture "is not failure, simply a realization that the venture no longer meets the needs of the entrepreneur." Ask whether a practice you intend to sustain indefinitely is a commitment or a trap, and what the honest exit from an artistic practice looks like.

## seminar order

A workable order for a three-hour session, with the passage to work from for each move.

- Open on the word itself. Say's coinage as "a declaration of dissent" (Andrews p. 1) against Rabideau's reclamation toward invention (p. 1). Get students to define it in their own words before either author does it for them.
- The history and the class question. Kolb pp. 9–11, the shift from skill to vision and the highbrow-lowbrow judgment. Include the periodization problem noted above.
- The two values. Kolb p. 11, aesthetic and monetary, and whether students can name a work whose value they hold entirely on one side.
- Disruption is optional. Andrews pp. 2–3, then his three counterexamples, then ask each student for a venture in their own field that adds rather than displaces.
- The mechanism, live. MVP, build-measure-learn, validated learning (Andrews pp. 5–6), applied to something each student could test within a month.
- The portraits. Rabideau pp. 6–9, one artist each, and the specific mechanism rather than the biography: the transcriptions sold, the liner notes written, the concert dress redesigned, the club series founded.
- The hard turn. McCurdy on the labor market and fear-based education. This is where the reflection prompt earns its second and third moves.
- Close on artistry and access. Doser pp. 10–11 and Musicambia p. 3, into the question of who the field's opportunities are currently built for, which is the thread week 5 picks up.

## into the component

The Creative Identity Statement is the week's Portfolio Component and what every later component refers back to. The readings supply four distinct entry points, and students should be pointed at whichever one moves them:

- McCurdy's "Defining Yourself as an Artist," the most direct match, plus the self-evaluation questions.
- Andrews's values exercise from the CCI workbook, which is the same first move the Values Statement formalizes in week 2 and the Mission and Vision Statements in week 3.
- Rabideau's four keys and the composer analogy, which ask what the big moments and transitions of a life's work look like.
- Kolb's Task 1a, "write in one sentence your passion in life," as the hardest short version.

The reflection prompt's three moves map onto the readings cleanly: define entrepreneurship (all four, and the differences between them are the substance), locate yourself (McCurdy's pipeline account and Rabideau's crossroads give students permission to be honest about stage), and name a challenged assumption (Drapkin, fear-based education, and the optional-disruption claim are the three that most often come up).
