# week 1 reading notes

Art, Culture & the Creative Career. Four chapters, one from each anchor and rotation text. They converge on two moves: reclaiming "entrepreneurship" from money-making toward invention and value, and putting the artist's own identity and values at the base of any venture. Summaries below are drawn from the chapters themselves.

## Kolb, ch. 1, "Art, culture and entrepreneurship" (pp. 8–30)

The business foundation of the course. Kolb's premise is that individual artists, creative industries, and cultural organizations have always been businesses, and the book exists to teach the entrepreneurship skills that arts training usually omits, so creatives can start a business, run a for-profit creative industry, or manage a nonprofit without a second job draining their time.

He opens historically. Society's definition of art has shifted: before the eighteenth century, art objects and performances were valued as expressions of a society's culture and were expected to be useful, and technical skill, not personal vision, was what made an artist. Only in the eighteenth century did vision and genius get added to skill as the marks of "true" art. From this he draws the aesthetic-versus-monetary distinction: art carries two values at once, and a buyer may pay for the beauty, the resale value, or both. He notes the highbrow-lowbrow split that emerged in the seventeenth century and the class judgment embedded in "dumbing down," and reminds the reader that Renaissance artists and Shakespeare's company were shrewd businesspeople who signed contracts and negotiated fees.

The chapter's throughline is that entrepreneurship is "just another means of creative expression." Technology and ecommerce now let creatives act as their own intermediary (the middle party, agent, gallery, or venue, that historically reached the audience and took a cut), promoting and selling directly. For both the creative and the entrepreneur, money is a means rather than the end: it pays the bills, buys materials, and buys back time to make the work. He then lays out the three organizational forms an arts venture can take (government, nonprofit, business), which differ by ownership, taxation, and purpose, and previews the four business structures that trade off risk and reward.

key ideas: art has two values, aesthetic and monetary; entrepreneurship as creative expression; the intermediary and the option to bypass it; money as a means; government/nonprofit/business as the three org forms. Two decisions Kolb plants for later: intermediary or direct, and nonprofit or business.

## Andrews, ch. 1, "What Is Arts Entrepreneurship?" (pp. 1–34)

The most thorough and academic of the four. Andrews maps the term from its standard definitions to its arts-specific form. He runs through Merriam-Webster (one who organizes, manages, and assumes the risks of an enterprise), Drucker (entrepreneurship as healthy change, doing something different rather than doing the same thing better), Say (who coined the word as a "declaration of dissent"), and Schumpeter ("creative destruction"), with the familiar disruption cases (Google, Netflix, Uber, Airbnb).

His pivotal claim is that arts entrepreneurs do not have to disrupt anything. A venture can find a home alongside, and in collaboration with, the organizations already in its field, and the founder is free to decide what the venture is based on, where it sits, and whom it serves. Where a venture begins, for Andrews, is with values: he walks through the Center for Cultural Innovation's "Business of Art" exercises, which move from exploring personal and professional values, to imagining a future life (a vision), to combining both into an actionable plan. He also notes that many arts founders build on real-world experience gained as an intern or employee first.

The chapter's distinctive contribution is the contrast between open-ended arts ownership and conventional entrepreneurship with an exit strategy. Many founders build in a "harvest" from day one, planning to go public, be acquired, or sell and move on (serial entrepreneurs). An arts entrepreneur can choose that path, but one building around a lifetime creative practice is usually after the opposite, an enterprise they intend to sustain indefinitely. Along the way he covers lean-startup ideas (the minimum viable product, testing early for validated learning), describing offerings by their benefits rather than features, the adoption curve (innovators and early adopters versus the majority), and the three kinds of risk an arts founder manages: financial, reputational, and legal.

key ideas: the textbook definitions and creative destruction; disruption is optional for artists; build the venture on your values (sets up weeks 2 and 3); open-ended commitment versus the exit strategy; MVP and validated learning; benefits over features; financial, reputational, and legal risk.

## Rabideau, ch. 1, "Being a 21st-Century Musician" (pp. 1–16)

The music-native manifesto and the spine of the book. Rabideau reclaims "entrepreneurship" from monetary gain toward the act of invention, and argues that artists and entrepreneurs are remarkably similar: both are curious, creative, collaborative, and tenacious, and both thrive in "the messy, fertile space of uncertainty, ambiguity, and promise." Entrepreneurs, he says, begin by problem-finding and ask "What can be?" rather than "What is?"

He positions musicians as creative agents of change against a narrow professional standard that has trained excellence while pushing creativity aside. He points to arts-entrepreneurship programs (Eastman, USC's Spark Lab, DePauw's 21st-Century Musician initiative) and to ventures that pursue impact: Decoda, and Musicambia's conservatories inside prisons. The recurring phrase is "a life of means and a life of meaning," money plus impact and giving back. A guest section by Greg Sandow, "What Is a 21st-Century Musician?", defines these artists by three habits: they go their own way, they perform or compose what they like (not always classical), and they make their own opportunities and shape their careers like entrepreneurs, in contrast to old rules where others controlled your performances and audience and your own identity mattered less. Portraits follow: Anderson & Roe (who arrange anything and sell their transcriptions), Stewart Goodyear (a single-day Beethoven-sonata marathon, and he writes his own liner notes), James Ross (empowering his students), Mason Bates (composer and DJ at once), Lara Downes (an "alt-classical" series and highly individual releases), and Yo-Yo Ma as the model artist-revolutionary.

A second guest section, James Doser's "Artistry at the Core," insists the mission is to add value to the lives of others through artistry, and that artistry is present "in all cultures, genders, ages, races, and economic strata." This is the chapter's clearest seed of the course's societal-impact and power-and-identity threads. Rabideau closes by naming the book's framework: five mindsets (unquenchable curiosity, purposeful creativity, strategic collaboration, passionate persistence, and critical optimism) and three skill sets (telling idea-driven stories, funding priceless ideas, and moving ideas to action), reduced to four keys (curiosity, creativity, collaboration, tenacity). He cites David Cutler's line that people see either one or two opportunities or a thousand, never five, and calls for divergent then convergent thinking. The revolutionary artist takes authority over their own artistry and path.

key ideas: entrepreneurship as invention; the artist as creative agent of change; make your own opportunities and define your own identity; "a life of means and a life of meaning"; artistry across all identities (the seed for outcomes 5 and 1); the five mindsets and three skills that structure the rest of the book.

## McCurdy, ch. 1, "A Model for Success"

Clear-eyed about the economy, warm about the work. The chapter opens with Herbie Hancock's "Art is what you do, not who you are," then spends its first section, "Defining Yourself as an Artist," on how artists see the world through a different lens and treat art as a reflection and interpretation of life, its love and anger, its triumphs and its darker histories of war and genocide. The "seagull syndrome" captures the always-working temperament of the artist.

McCurdy is blunt about the new arts economy. Traditional paths (touring big bands, steady orchestral jobs, full-time teaching, studio work, touring Broadway) have largely thinned, while arts training has expanded: a 2015 College Music Society report found a 123 percent rise in visual-and-performing-arts baccalaureate degrees between 1991 and 2011. The result is a supply-and-demand problem and a career lived project to project. He names a common trap, "fear-based education" (a phrase from jazz bassist John Clayton): defaulting to more school out of fear rather than a clear reason, and he presses students to weigh the real cost and return of an advanced degree.

His answer is a model. The twenty-first-century artist must be at once an entrepreneur, a storyteller, and an intellectual, curious and widely read, a critical thinker and a "citizen of the planet." Drawing on Covey's "be proactive," he frames the artist as an initiator and creator who builds a vision and makes things happen rather than waiting to be chosen, and he moves into setting and achieving goals.

key ideas: defining yourself as an artist (directly the week's component); the supply-and-demand reality of the arts economy; the "fear-based education" caution; the three qualities of the twenty-first-century artist; the proactive, initiating stance.

## how they fit together

Read together, the four give week 1 its arc. Kolb and Andrews supply the analytical frame (what entrepreneurship is, historically and in theory, and why an arts venture rests on the founder's values rather than on disruption). Rabideau and McCurdy supply the disposition and the mirror (the mindset of the self-directing artist, and the honest account of the economy that mindset has to work in). All four turn, by the end, to the same starting task: deciding who you are as an artist, which is what the Creative Identity Statement asks students to write. Rabideau's and McCurdy's insistence that artistry belongs to everyone, in every community, is the first appearance of the societal-impact and power-and-identity threads the course develops in weeks 5 and 15.
