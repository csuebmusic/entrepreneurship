# week 2 reading notes

Creative Identity, Voice & Values. Three chapters that turn last week's identity into something operational: the story only you tell (voice), the strengths a creative career actually runs on, and the values that decide what you take on. Summaries drawn from the chapters themselves.

## McCurdy, ch. 2, "What's the Story?"

McCurdy's premise is that artists are storytellers, and that a creative career depends on how convincingly you tell your story. The real subject is voice: "How do you find your creative voice? What is your story, your raison d'être? What are you trying to say?" He argues that voice rests on craft rather than replacing it: mastery of technique, vocabulary, and the history of the art is the ground reinvention stands on, and he uses Stravinsky's Rite of Spring and a list of artists "ahead of their time" (Coltrane, Picasso, van Gogh) to make the point that breaking the rules well requires knowing them first. He brings in Csikszentmihalyi's flow, the sense of time collapsing when a creator or an audience is fully absorbed, as a marker of strong storytelling.

The chapter is exercise-driven. Students list their own flow moments, as audience and as maker, and then build an "artistic DNA" by researching their family, roots, and personal stories. The underlying claim is that coming to a self-realization of who you are is a prerequisite for finding your artistic voice, and that what counts as great art is finally individual.

key ideas: artists as storytellers; voice as the story only you can tell; craft as the ground voice stands on; flow; self-knowledge and roots (the "artistic DNA" exercise) as the path to voice.

## Rabideau, ch. 3, "Assessing Your Strengths: Tenacity and the Superpowers" (pp. 31–42)

The argument is that you are more than your instrument. Musicians tend to define themselves narrowly (trombonist, vocalist), but a creative career runs on transferable strengths Rabideau calls superpowers: tenacity, problem-solving, communication, relationship-building, learning from others. He opens by contrasting school tests (fixed start and stop, right and wrong answers, consequences only for you) with real tests (complexity, ambiguity, consequences for others), and reframes failure through Stanford Thompson: the word once meant "cessation of supply," so failure is really the moment you stop supplying ideas.

The centerpiece is the flutist Sarah Robinson, who did everything "right" chasing a full-time orchestra job, hit audition after audition to the point of depression, and then built her own career after realizing there were "no walls." Her turn is to an internal compass, a short set of questions she checks her work against (Is it interesting? Am I supporting other artists? Am I helping make people's lives happier?), her own definition of success rather than the field's. Jennifer Rosenfeld's guest piece names six superpowers (wonder, imagination, critical optimism, tenacity, community, communication) and states plainly that they are "shaped by your values and beliefs."

key ideas: you are more than your craft; transferable strengths, or superpowers; failure as ceasing to supply ideas; the internal compass and a personal definition of success (the week's clearest values model); strengths shaped by values and beliefs.

## Rabideau, ch. 4, "Thinking about Creativity: Fueling the Imagination" (pp. 43–56)

Creativity, Rabideau argues, is where your unique voice enters, the "plus factor" that separates the careful from the courageous and infuses an interpretation with something only you would do. He surveys how creativity is under-fostered in music schools (improvisation and world-music work treated as ancillary rather than core), points to research on creativity across fields, then turns practical. His "creativity in your own words" exercise, defining the term yourself from many sources, doubles as a way to articulate your artistic life and even to draft a vision.

He offers a five-stage creative process (preparation, incubation, insight, evaluation, elaboration), stressing that it is iterative rather than linear, and pairs it with flow and David Cutler's line that people tend to have either one or two ideas or a thousand, rarely a handful. Mark Clague's guest piece on measured risk argues that risk cannot be fully measured or eliminated, so treat it as a guide: prepare hard, expand your target (the plural outcomes that define success), and count failure as failure only when you stop learning. The chapter also repeats the course's equity thread, that creative opportunity, and the hope it brings, matters most for those at the margins.

key ideas: creativity as voice (the "plus factor"); define creativity, and your artistic life, in your own words; the iterative five-stage process; measured risk and a plural definition of success; creative opportunity for those at the margins.

## how they fit together

The three move from voice to values and back. McCurdy sends you inward for the story only you can tell. Rabideau's strengths chapter turns that self-knowledge into an internal compass, values stated as questions you can check a decision against. His creativity chapter shows where voice actually enters the work and asks you to put your own terms to it. Together they set up the Values Statement, which asks students to name not the values they admire but the ones that actually govern their choices, stated so they could guide a real decision.
