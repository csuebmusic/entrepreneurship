# week 2 reading notes

Creative Identity, Voice & Values. McCurdy 2, Rabideau 3 (31–42), Rabideau 4 (43–56). Three chapters that turn week 1's identity into something operational: the story only you can tell, the strengths a creative career actually runs on, and the process by which a voice enters the work. Summaries and quotations are drawn from the chapters themselves. Page numbers are Rabideau's printed pages; McCurdy is a reflowable ebook and is cited by section heading and exercise number.

## the three in one line each

- **McCurdy 2** treats artists as storytellers, grounds voice in craft and self-knowledge, and runs five exercises from flow through genealogy and SWOT to a written artistic mission statement, then turns to attitude, grit, and the no-Plan-B claim.
- **Rabideau 3** argues you are more than your instrument, reframes failure as ceasing to supply ideas, and supplies the week's clearest values model in Sarah Robinson's internal compass and Rosenfeld's six superpowers, each defined.
- **Rabideau 4** locates voice in creativity, has you define creativity in your own words, and gives a five-stage iterative process from Csikszentmihalyi plus two guest essays on risk and on inspiration.

The three overlap on flow and on the ten-thousand-hours claim, so the seminar can treat them as one argument with three entrances.

## McCurdy, Goodstein & Lapin, ch. 2, "What's the Story?"

### the premise (section: Finding Your Soul)

"Artists are storytellers. Much of what we do is tell or interpret a story in a compelling manner." The list is deliberately cross-disciplinary: a Mozart symphony, painting a scene, acting, creative writing, working behind the scenes on a play, performing a ballet, interpreting a jazz standard, designing a set, writing a song. "You and your audience go on a shared journey. The success of a creative artist is often dependent on how convincingly you craft your story."

The chapter's four organizing questions, worth putting on the board as they are written: "How do you find your creative voice? What is your story, your raison d'être? How will you tell your story and what are you trying to say?"

### craft as the ground voice stands on

A claim to test in seminar: "One of the prerequisites for the creation of great art is mastery of the basic tools of the trade, flawless technique and the understanding of the vocabulary and history of art." The analogies are the carpenter who does not think twice about the hammer and the surgeon who knows every inch of your anatomy. "There are no shortcuts. If you haven't mastered the basics and understand the context and history, you won't have much success telling your story and subsequently great art will be beyond your reach."

Goodstein's marginal note supplies the aesthetic version: art at a high level "will lead an audience to understand the world in new or different ways," and it is personal experience plus technical mastery plus passionate delivery that makes the story convincing.

The worked case is the 1913 premiere of the *Rite of Spring*, which "broke so many conventional rules of the day, many in the audience were unable to process the artistic vision and a near riot famously broke out." The authors' reading: Stravinsky and Nijinsky knew their craft and were making a big idea. Their list of artists "ahead of their time" is John Coltrane, Frank Zappa, Picasso, the Velvet Underground, van Gogh, Bach, Frank Lloyd Wright, followed by an invitation to add your own.

The institutional argument follows: undergraduate arts training does the technical deep dive and the historical continuum well, and does not provide "opportunities to develop the aesthetic and creative tools necessary to build a sustainable career."

### flow (exercises 2.1 and 2.2)

Csikszentmihalyi's flow enters as the missing piece of creativity development: the sense that "time can seemingly stand still," available both to the maker immersed in the work and to the audience member for whom two hours pass in the blink of an eye.

- **Exercise 2.1:** three times you have been in the flow as an audience member.
- **Exercise 2.2:** three times you have been in the flow as a performer, writer, or artist.

The authors answer their own exercises, which is useful modelling for the seminar. Lapin lists the Ozawa recording of the *Rite of Spring* with the Chicago Symphony, *Swan Lake* by the Moscow City Ballet, and walking the Hermitage. Goodstein lists a Chicago Transit Authority concert as a teenager, and, tellingly for a non-artistic example, writing a complex business policy statement in what feels like minutes.

The chapter also notes the range of working temperaments rather than prescribing one: Beethoven struggled at every turn of phrase, Mozart conceptualized whole symphonies apparently effortlessly, some sculptors see the result in the block and others adjust as they go. The instruction that follows is the writing-teacher's: just start.

### the exercise sequence (2.3 through 2.5)

The chapter's frame.

- **Exercise 2.3, artistic DNA.** Gather your family history several generations back, write down the genealogy, annotate it, and collect personal stories. Methods offered: interviews with extended family, online genealogy resources, and, in Goodstein's note, consumer DNA testing as source material for the work. The stated payoff is a document that informs you as an artist. The underlying claim is stated twice for emphasis: "understanding who you are and where you come from will help you develop your skills as a creative artist."
- **Exercise 2.4, personal SWOT.** Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats, on a four-block worksheet of self-identified traits. The authors' warning is the interesting part: "once you dig into the details, you may find that some of your strengths are actually weaknesses and that may present some opportunities and challenges." They recommend a 360-degree version, sharing your self-identified traits with a partner, friend, or relative you trust completely and asking for their input.
- **Exercise 2.5, artistic mission statement.** Under 300 words, articulating "who you are as an artist, what is important to you, and what your artistic values are."

**A terminology collision to handle out loud.** The paragraph introducing Exercise 2.5 calls it a vision statement in its first sentence and a Mission Statement two sentences later, and the exercise itself calls it a mission statement. Our course separates these: the Creative Identity Statement was week 1, the Values Statement is this week, and Mission and Vision are distinct components in week 3. Name the difference when you assign the reading so students do not arrive at week 3 thinking they have already done it. McCurdy's Exercise 2.5 is closest to a first draft of the week 3 pair, and its three questions ("your overarching artistic values, what makes you distinctive, and your motivations") split cleanly across our Values Statement and the week 3 Mission.

McCurdy's own summary of the sequence: the SWOT, the genealogy, and the mission statement "will set you apart from the thousands of artists who have the talent, drive, and determination but lack an aesthetic core and a self-defined artistic vision."

### attitude, grit, and the no-Plan-B claim

The chapter's back half moves from voice to disposition, and it is more contestable than the front half.

- **Attitude.** "One trait that can set you apart beyond talent is a positive attitude." The evidence is thirty years of auditions for the Walt Disney All-American College program: talent is assessed in a few moments, and the rest of the audition time goes to intangibles, how a prospect reacts under pressure, what motivates them, red flags. "Our litmus test is to ask ourselves if we want to spend the summer with this prospect." The closing formulation: "Attitude is a decision and choice about how you present yourself, and one of the few skills you can control."
- **Grit.** "You should approach each rejection as not a 'no,' but a 'not yet.'" Lapin's orchestra-audition years supply the hard version: "in orchestral auditions, there is no prize for second place. Talent wasn't quite enough." A musician who auditioned for a military band position thirteen times before being hired is offered as the extreme case.
- **Passion as consumption.** "As artists, we need to have a deep and unwavering passion for our art. You can't fake it." Broadway performers doing the same role eight times a week are the model of professional passion, on the ground that "every audience is new at every performance."
- **No Plan B.** "If you choose to pursue this artistic life, it must consume your soul and every fiber of your existence." The authors qualify it twice: this does not mean forsaking food on the table or care for family, and day work as a server or driver is fine "as long as it is a means to an end, not 'making due.'" And then a real concession, easy to miss: "At some point, you will know if it's time for Plan B."

Jeff Coffin's contribution is the useful counterweight to the grit language, because it names cost rather than reward: "Being an artist, being a creator is about being brave. For me it's emotional. You have to be brave, you have to be willing to stand on that edge."

**Worth pressing in a graduate room.** The attitude and no-Plan-B material describes conditions of hiring as much as conditions of character. Ask whose "positive attitude" reads as positive to a panel, and what the "want to spend the summer with this prospect" test selects for beyond talent. That question is the honest bridge from this week's values work to week 5.

**Key terms introduced:** voice, raison d'être, flow, artistic DNA, SWOT, artistic mission statement, grit, not-yet rather than no.

## Rabideau, ch. 3, "Assessing Your Strengths: Tenacity and the Superpowers" (pp. 31–42)

### the claim (p. 31)

"We too often think of ourselves as trombonists, vocalists, or oboists. We are more. We are tenacious and complex and clever. We understand failure as a process, not an ending. And we are good at things like communicating with others, building relationships, solving problems, and learning from others' stories." The chapter's job is to surface "not-yet-known superpowers" and to reframe disappointment and failure as growth and opportunity.

### the truth about tests (pp. 31–32)

The frame that gets a graduate seminar talking, because it is about their training. Rabideau concedes on behalf of his colleagues that educators "have not prepared our graduates with the skills to be involved in the world," having focused on teaching students to take tests.

Three properties of tests, each with its reality:

- They have a start time and a stop time. Reality: most of life does not work this way.
- Generally there are right and wrong answers. Reality: "The real world is rich with complexity, ambiguity, and contradiction."
- They affect only one person, you. Reality: "No one lives in a bubble in the real world. Our actions always affect others."

And three things tests do not teach: how to pose a thoughtful question ("we are too busy pretending to have all of the answers"), how to ask for and get help, and how to use failure as a means to achieving goals.

His definition of a real test: "overcoming obstacles to achieve your goals," where what you learn is "an inevitable consequence of the act of invention."

### Stanford Thompson on failure (p. 33)

Guest section by the founder and executive director of Play On, Philly!, an El Sistema-inspired program, and a 2017 TED Fellow. His self-description, quoted, is a compact values statement in its own right and a good model for the week's component: "I believe that society thrives when artists prioritize the needs of their communities, so I create social harmony and opportunity through music."

The etymological argument: failure comes from the mid-seventeenth century and originally meant "nonoccurrence" and "cessation of supply." It has since come to mean "lack of success" and "an unsuccessful person, enterprise, or thing." Under the modern definition we avoid failure and read error as an internal deficiency. Thompson's restoration: "Failure is when you stop supplying ideas to complete the journey." Edison and the ten thousand attempts at the light bulb are the example.

His prompt, usable verbatim: remember a time you were part of an epic failure. What went wrong, and what would you do differently.

### Sarah Robinson's internal compass (pp. 33–35)

The chapter's centerpiece and the week's clearest working model of values. Robinson "did everything right": a conservatory orchestral studies program, technique honed with a flute guru until she was a monster player, her twenties and her money spent on winning a full-time orchestra job, "after all, you need that orchestra job to be a successful musician. At least, that's what Robinson believed."

Four passages from her Talk21 at DePauw's 2016 21CMposium, each doing distinct work:

- **On auditions.** "The more auditions I took, the worse and worse I played, because I felt so much pressure." Runner-up for a full-time job in Hawaii, and "runner-up, final rounds, they get you nothing. You go home empty-handed, minus all the money and time you've spent." Then: "I got depressed, and I felt terrible all the time," unable to get out of bed for two or three days after a bad audition.
- **On the turning point.** At the Blue Wisp Jazz Club in Cincinnati she saw Classical Revolution Cincinnati taking their music straight to their audience, booking their own clubs, choosing their own programs. "It was like I had spent 10 years banging my head against a door that would never open, only to look around and realize that there were no walls."
- **On what performance became.** "I really didn't care if I had the job that would prove I was a successful musician. I was too busy being a musician." And the consequence: "I wasn't a fearful performer anymore."
- **On success.** "I came to my own definition of success. And I would check my internal compass against what I was doing to see if it was successful." The three questions she checks against: Is it interesting? Am I supporting other artists? Am I helping to make people's lives a little happier and a little bit more fulfilling? "And those questions are how I decide what I do next in my career."

**This is the model to hand students for the Values Statement.** Three questions, phrased so they decide something, checked against actual work. Not a list of admired qualities. The assignment is essentially asking each student to write their own version of Robinson's three.

### Rosenfeld's six superpowers (pp. 35–37)

Guest section by Jennifer Rosenfeld, CEO of iCadenza and president of Cadenza Artists. The origin story is worth keeping: she and her partner Julia were senior music majors, unsure the performance track was right, who gathered how-to books, wrote a business plan, entered it in a competition on a whim, and placed second. "The first two years were brutal! The confidence we had gained writing that business plan fell like a house of cards when it became painfully clear how little we really knew. But we had tenacity." Her conclusion is the chapter's thesis in another voice: what it takes is not a PhD or a secret formula but "the repetition of core behaviors that we had already developed through our years as students and musicians."

The definition that makes this a values week rather than a skills week: "Your artistry and ideas are the substance, and 'superpowers' are the vehicles for achieving your mission, the unique abilities that exist at the intersection of passion, skill, and drive. 'Superpowers' are shaped by your values and beliefs as well as your professional habits."

The six, with her definitions:

- **Wonder:** curiosity mixed with a driving impulse to seek out problems worth solving.
- **Imagination:** devising unique solutions to observed challenges.
- **Critical optimism:** believing we can make a difference in the world, if we create a feasible plan of action.
- **Tenacity:** polite persistence in the face of challenges.
- **Community:** assembling the support and collaboration needed to bring an idea to fruition.
- **Communication:** expressing meaningful content in a way that resonates with others.

Her tenacity entry names the conditions plainly: "we were young and inexperienced; the industry was shrinking and closed-minded, and we were women in a male-dominated field." The result of polite persistence was champions, "the people who admired our stick-to-itiveness, and even offered to help." This is a second point where the week's material opens onto week 5.

A Superpowers Assessment is referenced at 21CM.org. Check whether the link still resolves before pointing students to it.

### Matt McBane and the Carlsbad Music Festival (pp. 37–42)

The chapter's extended case, and the one that turns values into an institution. McBane founded the festival at 23 with "no real prior experience other than organizing a few musical concerts in college."

- **The origin.** "The Carlsbad Music Festival was born out of dissatisfaction and love, dissatisfaction with the musical world that was presented to me, and love of the music I was learning about in and out of school." Living in Los Angeles after graduation he found no organizations nurturing an early-career composer and little chance to hear his peers' work outside academia. Bang on a Can's Summer Institute showed him "that composers with a vision and dissatisfaction with the status quo could change the musical landscape."
- **Year one.** Two concerts in a weekend plus school outreach in his hometown, with friends from USC including the Calder Quartet as founding ensemble in residence, supported by a risk-taking grant from the City of Carlsbad Arts Office. He did every job: programming, program layout, writing a major new piece, grant writing, marketing, setting up chairs.
- **Where it got to.** Four part-time employees, two annual events, more than sixty performances each, roughly 4,500 audience members per event and 9,000 annually. Praise from the *Los Angeles Times* and from Alex Ross. Premieres by Wu Man, David Lang, Steven Schick; emerging musicians championed including Jennifer Bewerse, Scott Worthington, Trouble in the Wind.
- **The pivot, and its price.** Six years in a library auditorium in a strip mall, a venue "that felt appropriate to only certain genres." In 2010 he tried a Village Music Walk in businesses and public spaces of the historic core, inspired by Brooklyn DIY venues and by Big Ears in Knoxville. It worked, and the next year the entire festival moved, "forgoing $12,000 in funding tied to the library venue and moving from the known to the unknown (much to the consternation of our board)."
- **Mission discovered rather than declared.** Once in the Village, two words emerged as the description of the festival's mission, musicians, and audience: **adventurous** and **community**. "I didn't realize when I founded the festival, but as the organization has developed, I see that these two concepts have been through lines from the get-go, and they were only allowed to fully bloom with this venue move."
- **The advice.** "If you want to start an organization, a crucial thing is to know clearly why you want to start it. What do you feel is lacking in your musical world that a new organization can change? What are the things you love in your musical world that a new organization can better serve?" And the claim underneath: "musical culture is not a fixed thing that has to be handed down to us from above, but is something to which we all contribute and help shape."

His six-question "Create Your Festival" list (pp. 41–42) works as a values instrument even for students with no interest in founding anything: what do you love and want to share; what are you dissatisfied with and want to change; what kind of artistic life do you want; which artists and people do you want to work with; what like-minded organizations could you partner with; who are your dream collaborators.

**Chapter summary (p. 42):** "Tenacity is likely the most valuable attribute you possess ... Your tenacity is what pushes you to seek something that currently exists only at the edge of your imagination."

**Key terms introduced:** superpowers, the internal compass, failure as cessation of supply, wonder, imagination, critical optimism, tenacity, community, communication.

## Rabideau, ch. 4, "Thinking about Creativity: Fueling the Imagination" (pp. 43–56)

### creativity as the plus factor (pp. 43–44)

The opening formula: "Curiosity drives us to ask questions. Creativity helps us answer them."

Where creativity enters performance: mastering complex passages through multiple approaches, or "breathing life anew into a piece by crafting an interpretation that both embodies the history of the work and infuses our unique voice." Then the definition by contrast, three pairs in a row: creativity "is the line that distinguishes the careful from the courageous, the impressive from the impassioned, and the memorable from the indelible." The term Rabideau borrows for it, from Elizabeth Hinckley's 21CM.org piece "The Art (+ Science) of 'It,'" is **the plus factor**.

### the curricular argument (p. 44)

Rabideau's account of where creativity currently sits in music training, which a graduate seminar will have opinions about:

- Electives such as "Improvisation for Classical Musicians" ask students to see themselves as spontaneous composers.
- Arrangement and composition assignments inside theory sequences give a glimpse of the composer's mind, but are "few and far between" and do not build the chops to move past a functional assignment.
- World music and new music ensembles supply diverse cultural norms, access to composers' intentions, unfamiliar instruments, notational systems, extended techniques. "Everything about these experiences exudes creativity, yet these ensembles are most often 'in addition to' rather than the 'officially approved' ensembles for credit, band, choir, orchestra."

His position: "creativity can thrive anywhere," and experiences that stretch the imagination "must become core, not ancillary, curricular requirements."

He also gestures at the research literature across fields, naming Berkowitz's *The Improvising Mind*, Haworth and Colton's *Gender, Age, and Musical Creativity*, Cope's *Computer Models of Musical Creativity*, and Demers's *Steal This Music*, from psychology, gender studies, computer science, and law respectively.

### Bruce Adolphe as the diverse career (p. 45)

Composer with premieres by Yo-Yo Ma, Itzhak Perlman, and Joshua Bell; author of three books; faculty at Juilliard, Yale, and NYU; composer in residence at the Brain and Creativity Institute; the Piano Puzzler on American Public Media's *Performance Today*; founder and director of Lincoln Center's family series Meet the Music. The point of the profile is range held together by one artistic identity, which is the question the portfolio's coherence requirement asks of students.

### define creativity in your own words (pp. 45–47)

The chapter's central exercise, and its second function is the one that matters for us: hunting definitions across sources "helps craft a vision statement for one's artistic life." The questions it generates, in Rabideau's list: how do I move past my comfort zone, how do I embrace new approaches to performance (improvisation, global music influences, interdisciplinary collaborations), how can I think more creatively and act innovatively.

The definitions he assembles, useful as a set to run in seminar:

- **Rabideau:** "creativity is the wondrous space between things familiar and things yet imagined." He tests it against a pop song whose form, harmony, melodic shape, and rhyme scheme you can predict on first hearing (not creative, because predictable), and against something so foreign there is no path to follow ("creativity is simply chaos"). He then concedes the flaw: listeners with bigger ears than his will trace the thought driving highly complex music and find it creative, so the gap sits in a different place for them.
- **Wikipedia:** "a phenomenon whereby something new and somehow valuable is formed." His objection: sterile, and short on the arts.
- **New Encyclopaedia Britannica:** "the ability to make or otherwise bring into existence something new, whether a new solution to a problem, a new method or device, or a new artistic object or form." His objection: this is closer to innovation, since it moves creative thought into action with a clear outcome.
- **Gamin**, who performs on traditional Korean wind instruments with the Contemporary Gugak Orchestra at the National Gugak Center: creative work must be "honest and very personal," and must reflect the individual above all.
- **Melissa Snoza** of Fifth House Ensemble, quoted at length and the most useful of the set for students who do not see themselves as originators: "There's a person like Dan Visconti who can create something from nothing ... For me, creativity is less about making something from nothing than about collage. I'm the kind of person where creativity means seeing the connections between really disparate things that other people don't see." At the organizational level her creativity is finding the nexus of a piece someone wants to play, a partner who came into the orbit, and a problem in the world, and turning it into a project. She closes on the ensemble needing "creativity of many different kinds."

The **creativity against innovation** distinction that emerges here is worth making explicit and holding for the rest of the term: creativity generates the idea, innovation moves it into action with an outcome.

### the five-stage process (pp. 48–51)

From Csikszentmihalyi's *Creativity: Flow and the Psychology of Discovery and Invention*.

1. **Preparation.** "Becoming immersed in problematic issues that are interesting and arouse curiosity." For musicians, the practice room, or David Taylor's "embracing the joy of the struggle." Csikszentmihalyi's ten-year domain requirement is cited alongside Gladwell's ten-thousand-hour rule. Rabideau's jazz illustration is the sharpest passage in the chapter: transcribing solos, copying inflection, memorizing standard phrases puts an emerging artist in close proximity to creative output, but "this is by definition re-creative, not creative." Creativity begins when they **stretch**, going "for something they have never heard, never played, in search of alternate chords over standard changes," or crafting a sound that is uniquely their own. He names the line this draws between Ellington, Parker, Davis, and Fitzgerald and "those of us still in search of our voices," with Davis on "Someday My Prince Will Come" and Coltrane on "My Favorite Things" as the examples. He also notes he wishes there were a classical-music parallel for the word *stretch*, which is itself a good seminar question.
2. **Incubation.** Ideas "churn around below the threshold of consciousness." His practical complaint: our days are not structured to leave time to sit with thoughts, and we fill silence with commitments and distractions, "some important, some urgent, and many neither important nor urgent."
3. **Insight.** "The Aha! moment when the puzzle starts to fall together." He deflates the muse: more likely this is the moment your logged time at the craft collides with your exploration of the space between the familiar and the yet imagined, "even if it is incomplete." It happens in the car, on a run, in the shower, asleep.
4. **Evaluation.** "Deciding if the insight is valuable and worth pursuing." His method is to say it out loud to trusted colleagues, on the grounds that hearing your own words changes how you process validity, feasibility, importance, and impact. This is also, he says, the stage that separates people who enjoy imagining ideas from innovators. It is the direct argument for the seminar's workshop format.
5. **Elaboration.** "Translating the insight into its final work," where ideas move "from vision to fruition, creativity to innovation, concepts to action."

Stated explicitly at p. 51: "although presented here linearly, the creative process is iterative, continually circling back through each stage."

**The equity claim, embedded at p. 50 and easy to read past.** "It is my belief that creativity is the defining line between desperation and hope. With no way of imagining a better life, we are only left with our current reality. For those living at the edge of desperation, knowing that they can reimagine their own existence and be an agent for change in their own world makes way for hope. This is why providing creative opportunities for everyone is crucial, with special attention to those at the margins." This is the second appearance of the thread Doser opened in week 1, and the closest Rabideau comes to an argument for access.

**Peter Seymour** on PROJECT Trio (p. 50) supplies the biographical case for elaboration: three players in their late twenties chasing orchestral careers and unhappy, "on the verge of being out of the music business completely," who realized "if we didn't create something that was our own, then we were going to get pushed out of the business," and went full force into making music, producing videos, and everything a full-time ensemble requires.

### Mark Clague, "Taking Measured Risks" (pp. 51–52)

Guest section by a musicologist at Michigan who directs Entrepreneurship and Career Services there. Four moves:

- Risk is inseparable from action. "To do something is inherently dangerous. You might fail and (maybe just as threatening to your comfort zone) you might succeed."
- Measurement fails. "To measure risk before it is taken is ultimately impossible. Only zero risk can be known." Trying to control it "is to let risk become the focus, diminishing your possibilities of success."
- Risk as guide rather than obstacle: it "will galvanize your fears and help you identify the skills, resources, and vision needed to succeed."
- **Expand the target.** "Expand your target area, your goals, the outcomes (plural) that define your success. Understand that aiming for the pinnacle means falling short much of the time, but that failing to reach the summit is still to ascend far up the mountainside." Then the definition that pairs with Thompson's: "failure is only that if you fail to learn, and if you stop trying to make a difference."

Clague's plural outcomes and Robinson's three questions are the two mechanisms in the week that actually operationalize values. Put them side by side.

### Bruce Adolphe, "Moonlight and Chocolate: Finding Inspiration" (pp. 52–55)

The chapter's closing guest essay, practical for students who want to know what to do on Monday morning.

- Inspiration "may come from anywhere: moonlight, mountains, mathematics, a phrase of Mozart, birdsong, a crack of thunder, a starry night, dark chocolate, a stranger's glance," and the requirement is to be "willing and prepared, open and ready."
- The mechanism: "Inspiration is the connecting of experience to emotional memory and to imagination." Emotional memories are felt in the body and relived. "Memory and imagination work together."
- The core exercise: start with any memory, recall it in as much detail as possible, then let the image evolve on its own into something new, and notice how it feels to let it change without your mind seeming to control it.
- **The Perlman anecdote**, which is the essay's proof. Perlman commissioned a solo violin work about food, with poems by Louise Gikow. Presented with a movement called "Brandy," Perlman said he does not really like brandy and would prefer to think of the lyrical piece as chocolate cake, then "immediately began to play the piece with a deliciously sticky chocolate legato." The title stuck. Adolphe's point: "There are many kinds of legato a violinist can employ, but a legato inspired by chocolate is bound to be unique, personal, and sound inspired." The technique follows the image.
- The reverse direction, for repertoire where the image does not fit: play a Bach sarabande without thinking about technique, let the music stimulate emotional memory, actually feel what surfaces rather than indicating it, and let memory reveal its source. "From there, you will find that technique is about expression, not something separate."
- His own case: composing for the film *Einstein's Light*, taking phrases of Mozart and Bach that Einstein played and loved and imagining them "floating, bending, shifting, and transforming as if they were part of one of Einstein's remarkable thought experiments," while disclaiming any grasp of the mathematics. "The sense of wonder sparked by the feeling of the thought experiment was really all I needed."
- The summary line: "it is connections that inspire us," and "technique should serve the imagination."

**"Find Your Muse" exercises (pp. 54–55).** Scenarios to imagine before playing the same Bach sarabande, each setting a different emotional environment. Two of the five are hypothetical audiences under extreme duress, prisoners condemned to death and captors who will spare your life if moved to tears. Preview them before assigning if you plan to work them in seminar. The remaining three transfer cleanly to any discipline: playing an enchanted instrument that performs without your control, playing for a single depressed patient who has not spoken in days, and playing a phrase-by-phrase increase in joy you have been convinced is the composer's intent.

The scale exercises that close the chapter transpose to any medium and are the fastest way into this material in a live session: perform your most routine technical exercise as if you hate it, as if you were a robot with no emotions ("this is tricky. Feeling detached or bored is not without emotion. Is it possible to do this?"), as if you have just recovered from paralysis of the hands, and as if you truly believe it is a wonderful piece of music.

**Chapter summary (p. 55):** "We are artists, not simply instrumentalists; creators, not just folks who re-create the work of others; and central players in the reinvention of music for the 21st century."

**Key terms introduced:** the plus factor, stretch, re-creative against creative, creativity against innovation, preparation, incubation, insight, evaluation, elaboration, measured risk, expanded target, emotional memory.

## where the three converge

- **Voice is discovered rather than declared.** McCurdy sends you into genealogy and SWOT before any statement is written. Rabideau's Robinson finds her compass only after ten years of pursuing someone else's definition. McBane's mission words, adventurous and community, surface after seven years and only become visible once the venue changes. Nobody in this week's reading names their values first and then acts.
- **Craft is the precondition, and all three say so.** McCurdy: no shortcuts, master the vocabulary and history. Rabideau ch. 4: preparation is stage one, ten years in the domain, and imitation is re-creative until you stretch. Both cite Gladwell's ten thousand hours, and Rabideau made the same caveat in week 1.
- **Flow appears in both books.** McCurdy uses it as a marker of successful storytelling, in the audience and in the maker. Rabideau uses Csikszentmihalyi for the process framework and points to the 2004 TED Talk. Same source, two different jobs.
- **Failure is redefined twice, compatibly.** Thompson: failure is when you stop supplying ideas. Clague: failure is only failure if you fail to learn and stop trying to make a difference. McCurdy's "not yet" is the same move in plainer language.
- **Values are stated as questions rather than nouns.** Robinson's three questions, McBane's six, McCurdy's mission-statement prompt, and Clague's plural outcomes are all instruments for deciding rather than lists of virtues. This is exactly what the Values Statement asks for.

## where they conflict

1. **Is voice found by looking inward or by looking outward?** McCurdy's route is genealogy, roots, family stories, self-realization. Rosenfeld's superpowers were discovered by talking to many musicians about the challenges they face, and Snoza's creativity is finding connections between disparate things outside herself. Ask students which route their own practice actually uses, and whether the inward route risks producing a statement nobody else needs.
2. **Attitude as character or as gatekeeping.** McCurdy presents a positive attitude as a controllable skill and the deciding factor in hiring. Rosenfeld names being young, inexperienced, and a woman in a male-dominated field as the conditions her tenacity had to work against. Both accounts of "what gets you hired" are honest; they are not the same account.
3. **Craft first, or permission first.** McCurdy: without mastery "great art will be beyond your reach." Robinson had the mastery and it did not help until she stopped asking permission. Rabideau holds both, which makes his position the one to interrogate: is preparation a gate, or a resource you draw on whenever you start?
4. **Whose definition of creativity governs.** Rabideau builds the exercise on the premise that you should define it yourself, then supplies a definition that he immediately admits is flawed and observer-dependent. Snoza's collage version and Gamin's honesty-and-personal version do not reduce to his gap between familiar and yet imagined. The productive question is whether a definition you write for yourself can do any work, or whether it only describes what you already do.
5. **No Plan B against expanded targets.** McCurdy asks for total consumption with a single goal in the crosshairs. Clague asks you to expand the target area into plural outcomes. These are opposite instructions about the same anxiety. Students in a graduate program are living this one now.

## seminar order

A workable order for a three-hour session, with the passage to work from for each move.

- Open on the tests passage (Rabideau pp. 31–32). Ask what their training prepared them for and what it did not. It is about them, it costs nothing to answer, and it sets the honesty level for the night.
- Failure, redefined. Thompson's etymology (p. 33) and his epic-failure prompt. Then Clague's "failure is only that if you fail to learn" (p. 52) as the pair.
- The long case: Sarah Robinson (pp. 33–35). Read the four excerpts in order. Land on the three questions and hold them on the board for the rest of the session.
- Voice and its sources: McCurdy's four questions, then the artistic DNA exercise. Have them name one thing in their roots that shows up in the work whether or not they intended it.
- Superpowers as values in disguise: Rosenfeld's six with her definitions (pp. 35–37), and her sentence that superpowers are "shaped by your values and beliefs."
- Values that built something: McBane (pp. 37–42). Focus on the venue move, the $12,000 forgone, the board's consternation, and the two words that surfaced afterward. Ask what decision of theirs would have to change if their stated values were real.
- Creativity as the place voice enters: the plus factor and the stretch passage (pp. 43–44, 48–49). Run the definition exercise live, three minutes in writing, then read several around the room against Rabideau's, Snoza's, and Gamin's.
- Close in the body: Adolphe's chocolate legato (p. 53) and the scale exercises (p. 55). One round of the routine-technical-exercise exercise in whatever their medium is, if the Zoom format allows.

## into the component

The Values Statement asks for values "stated so they could guide an actual decision." The readings supply four instruments, and students should pick the one that fits how they actually think:

- **Robinson's internal compass** (Rabideau pp. 34–35). Three questions, checked against work in progress. The closest match to the assignment, and the model to lead with.
- **McBane's six questions** (Rabideau pp. 41–42). What you love and want to share, and what you are dissatisfied with and want to change. Best for students whose values are clearer as grievances than as principles.
- **McCurdy's SWOT and Exercise 2.5** (McCurdy ch. 2). For students who want a structured inventory before writing. Flag the mission-statement terminology so it does not preempt week 3.
- **Clague's expanded target** (Rabideau p. 52). Plural outcomes that define success. Useful for anyone whose stated values collapse into a single job title.

The reflection prompt asks for two or three values that actually govern creative decisions with a real example of each, and one way to enforce that is Robinson's structure: if the value cannot be phrased as a question you could ask about a piece of work, it is not yet a value, it is an adjective. That test is worth saying out loud before they write.

## outcome trace

Week 2 traces to MUS 603 outcome 3: identify and articulate personal career paths in the arts, employing tools such as résumés, cover letters, and online media.

The articulation half is this week's part of it. Voice, strengths, and values are the material a career path gets articulated from, and Robinson's chapter is a career path being re-identified in real time. The named tools in the outcome are assigned in week 4, so week 2 and week 4 together satisfy it.
