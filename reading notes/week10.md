# week 10 reading notes

Pricing & Money, Movement III continued. Four readings on setting a price and defending it, plus the access dimension the course adds on top of the readings. Summaries drawn from the chapters.

## NYFA, ch. 13, "Pricing Your Art" (pp. 206–215)

Pricing across disciplines. NYFA starts from cost, the materials, tools, and time that give you a baseline to recoup, then makes the key move: the value of your work is a function of the market for your medium and your specific position in that market, which the earlier market-research chapter helps you read. It argues that well-considered prices signal professionalism. The rest is discipline-specific. For visual artists: galleries generally do not lower a list price (discounts are handled instead, shared up to ten percent), so price realistically from the start and, if you are emerging, compare against work of similar size and materials by an artist at a similar career stage. For performing artists: there is less risk in adjusting prices, products like recordings sit in a fairly established market (single CDs and downloads in a roughly $10–25 range), and the value of a live performance ends with the performance. (No sliding-scale or access pricing appears in this chapter.)

key ideas: cost as a baseline; value as market plus position; compare by career stage; discipline norms.

## Kolb, ch. 6, "Mastering the numbers" (pp. 128–150)

The methods chapter. Kolb separates revenue from profit and lays out three pricing approaches: cost (price to cover fixed and variable costs plus a margin), competition (price against comparable products), and prestige (price high to signal exclusivity). He is careful about the word value: in business it means worth measured against the benefits received, not cheapness, and he warns that a low price can actively signal low quality, which is why underpricing to make a sale is a classic startup mistake ("you're worth it"). He supplies the break-even formula, FC / (P − VC), for the number of sales a given price needs to cover costs, and closes with cash management, income tracking by product line, and diversification, since more than one revenue stream both raises income and cushions the loss of any one.

key ideas: cost, competition, and prestige approaches; value as worth not cheapness; underpricing as the common error; break-even; diversification.

## Landry, ch. 3, "Create with Constraints" (pp. 53–74)

Read here for the budgeting section, which is the ground a cost-based price stands on. Landry walks through building an initial budget from materials and help, and draws the fixed-versus-variable line clearly: fixed costs (rent, insurance, hosting, subscriptions) hold no matter how much you produce, while variable costs (travel, materials, freelance help, utilities) rise with output. He adds the basics of bookkeeping and accounting, including that tracked expenses can be tax-deductible, so you know what your work actually costs to make before you price it.

key ideas: build the budget first; fixed versus variable costs; bookkeeping as the source of your true cost.

## McCurdy, ch. 4, "It's Not about the Money—Yet!" (chapter)

The money mindset. McCurdy rejects the starving-artist premise: a professional artist should expect wages like any other professional, and the chapter's running examples (playing a reception for "punch and cookies," a college gig for a hot dog and a Coke) are aimed at the habit of undervaluing creative work. Their test for when pay is warranted is demand: when people are asking for your talents, you are worth paying, whether or not you feel you have reached excellence. The title's "yet" carries the counterweight: in the earliest developmental stage, money should not be the measure, and craft, aspiration, and creative integrity come first.

key ideas: reject undervaluation; demand as the signal that pay is warranted; early on, development over money.

## how they fit together

The four support the reflection's three approaches. Cost-based pricing runs on Landry's budget and Kolb's cost method and break-even. Market-based pricing is Kolb's competition approach and NYFA's market-and-position reading, compared by career stage. Value-based pricing is Kolb's value (worth against benefit, not cheapness), NYFA's position, and the prestige case. On mindset, Kolb and McCurdy converge on underpricing as the recurring error, with McCurdy's nuance that early development is not the moment to optimize for money. The course adds one dimension the readings do not: access. A price is also a gate, deciding who can afford the work, which ties pricing back to the positionality and access work of week 5; established practices like a sliding scale, pay-what-you-can, or a community or student rate let an artist hold a price and open a door at once. That is the Pricing Rationale (outcomes 2 and 4): a price for at least one offering, the reasoning behind it, and how it handles access. The reflection has students price one offering three ways and defend one.
