# week 5 reading notes

Power, Social Identity & Access. The one week the six course texts could not cover alone. Rabideau 7 gives the individual-bias-and-inclusion angle plus one case; the two outside readings supply the structural evidence across gender and class. Both outside sources were checked as real and openly available before assigning (citations and links below).

## Rabideau, ch. 7, "Diversity and Inclusivity: Abandoning Bias and Listening to Voices Unlike Our Own" (pp. 73–84)

Written by Laura Hlavacek Rabideau, an HR executive with a flute-performance background, so the lens is organizational and individual rather than structural. She defines diversity (all the ways we differ: race, ethnicity, gender and gender identity, sexual orientation, age, religion, disability, socioeconomic status, education, cognitive style) and inclusion, quoting Andrés Tapia: "diversity is the mix, inclusion is making the mix work." The engine of the chapter is implicit bias, the subconscious assumptions we act on without noticing, which she argues can be unlearned through introspection. Her central case is the blind audition: US top-five orchestras were 95 percent men in the 1970s and about 25 percent women by 1997, and a screen in the preliminary round raised a woman's chance of advancing by roughly 50 percent. The rest of the chapter moves through the inclusive team (a jazz trio as the model), leadership under discrimination ("Be the Boss," built on Beyoncé), community engagement done with rather than to a community, and access organizations (the Sphinx Organization, Project STEP, the Ritz Chamber Players) that open classical music to musicians of color. The chapter's own framing is invitational: it opens by telling the reader the discussion may feel uncomfortable and to embrace that.

The gap: it is music-focused and pitched at the level of individual bias and team culture, not the structural history of who has been able to build an arts career. The outside readings fill that.

## Goldin & Rouse (2000), "Orchestrating Impartiality: The Impact of 'Blind' Auditions on Female Musicians," American Economic Review 90(4), 715–741

The study behind Rabideau's blind-audition example, and the scholarly source. Using actual audition records from major US orchestras in a fixed-effects design, Goldin and Rouse find that a screen raises the probability a woman advances out of certain preliminary rounds by about 50 percent, and that the shift to blind auditions explains between 30 and 55 percent of the increase in the share of women among new hires since 1970 (women went from under 5 percent of the top-five orchestras to about 25 percent). Open access via NBER (working paper 5903) and Harvard DASH. Worth knowing: the causal claim has since been publicly contested, notably a 2019 statistical critique (Andrew Gelman, and Christina Hoff Sommers in the press) and a 2017 Australian public-service study that failed to replicate the effect. That contest is a feature for a graduate seminar, not a problem: it lets students read a canonical "fix" as argued-over rather than settled. Focus for students: the introduction and the results.

## Brook, O'Brien & Taylor (2018), "Panic! Social Class, Taste and Inequalities in the Creative Industries," Create London

The structural, cross-disciplinary piece, and the widest lens of the four. Billed as the first sociological study of social mobility into creative work, it takes an intersectional approach with class as the primary axis and gender and ethnicity intersecting. Four parts. Part 1: a high share of creative workers believe the sector is a meritocracy of talent and hard work, and the people most attached to that belief, and least likely to see class, gender, or ethnicity as barriers, are the best-paid, disproportionately white men, the very people positioned to change things. Part 2: the workforce is markedly unrepresentative, with low shares of workers from working-class origins and low shares of Black and minority-ethnic workers in specific sectors (2.7 percent in museums, galleries, and libraries; 4.2 percent in film, TV, and radio; 4.8 percent in music, performing, and visual arts), a gender pay gap of about £5,800 a year overall (up to £15,000 in film and TV), and a class pay gap of up to £23,000 in publishing. There was "no golden age": the chances of entering by social origin stayed roughly stable from 1981 to 2011, but as Britain's class structure shifted, the share of young cultural workers from upper-middle-class origins more than doubled (15 to 33 percent) while the working-class share dropped by about a third. Part 3: unpaid labour is endemic (around 87 percent had worked for free), and it is class-divided, a manageable choice for the affluent and a barrier or form of exploitation for those without family support. Part 4: creative workers hold distinct tastes and values, and hiring can work as "cultural matching" that excludes those who do not share them. UK data, open access via Create London. Focus for students: the introduction and Parts 1 and 2.

## how they fit together

Rabideau names the problem at the level of the individual (bias, inclusion) and gives one vivid case. The two outside readings supply the structure and the numbers, across identities and disciplines: Goldin and Rouse on gender in music (a fix that worked, and is now debated), and Panic! on class across the whole creative sector (the meritocracy story against the workforce reality). Together they move the week from "bias exists" to "here is how power and social identity have distributed access, with evidence," which is what outcomes 1 and 5 ask for. They also ground the two pieces of student work: the reflection (how these forces shaped access in the student's own discipline) and the durable Positionality and Access Reflection (the student's own position, access, and barriers), the keeper the rest of the term refers back to.
