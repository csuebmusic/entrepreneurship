# week 14 reading notes

Financial Sustainability & Funding, in Module V. Four readings on two questions: what sustaining the practice costs (money in and out) and where the money comes from (a funding path). Summaries drawn from the chapters.

## NYFA, ch. 6, "Budgets" (pp. 109–116)

The budgeting chapter. NYFA's argument is that budgeting matters most for the smallest practices, where every penny counts, and it lays out the two basic kinds: a project budget (covering every stage of a project, and often required for a grant or an investor) and an annual budget (all your income from every source set against a complete summary of your costs for the year). It runs through who needs a budget, when, how to build one, and why it matters.

key ideas: budgets matter most when resources are small; the project budget versus the annual budget; the annual budget as the whole year's money in against money out.

## NYFA, ch. 7, "Financial Terminology" (pp. 117–135)

The vocabulary of financial health. NYFA walks the core financial documents: the income statement (revenue minus expenses, the basic read on whether a practice is actually sustaining itself), the balance sheet, and the handling of taxes. It is the reference for the terms the rest of the week uses.

key ideas: the income statement as revenue minus expenses; the balance sheet; taxes; the terms that describe a practice's finances.

## Rabideau, ch. 11, "Finding the Funds: Donations, Grants, and Finance Management" (pp. 133–146)

The funding chapter, by Steven Linville. It pairs building a realistic budget (list every possible expense, leave nothing out, and look for savings in the people already involved) with securing revenue, and its central idea is that most projects run on a portfolio of revenue sources rather than one. He splits them into what you earn (ticket sales, fees) and what you raise (grants, donations, membership fees, board contributions, crowdsourcing), and stresses that not every source fits every project, since your status (independent, sponsored, nonprofit) and focus determine which doors are open. On grants specifically: research whether your work actually fits the funder, read the application and its requirements and deadlines closely, make a timeline, and lead with a clear need statement.

key ideas: a realistic, all-in budget; revenue as a portfolio of earned and raised sources; fit between the source and the project; grants reward research and preparation.

## Kolb, ch. 10, "Financial concepts for success" (pp. 224–248)

The management chapter. Kolb's point is that mastering production and marketing is not enough: a venture can still fail without financial skills, so an entrepreneur has to read financial statements, manage cash flow, handle payments, and negotiate contracts. The discipline he emphasizes for this week is separation: keep business finances apart from personal ones, with separate accounts, so you can compare what the practice earns against what it spends on its own terms, claim business expenses against tax, and avoid the trap of mistaking activity for profit. He also covers banking relationships and processing payments.

key ideas: financial skills as necessary for survival; separating business from personal finances; not mistaking activity for profit.

## how they fit together

The four fall into the week's two questions. Money in and out is NYFA's annual budget, NYFA's income statement, and Kolb's discipline of tracking business finances separately, which together give a clear picture of what a practice earns and spends. The funding path is Rabideau's portfolio of earned and raised sources, fitted to the kind of practice you run. Sustainability is where the two meet: a realistic year in which the money in can cover the money out, drawn from a funding mix that actually fits. That is the Financial Snapshot (outcome 2), which builds on the Business Model Snapshot of week 9 and the Pricing Rationale of week 10. The reflection asks what sustaining the practice for a year would take, and where the funding could come from.
