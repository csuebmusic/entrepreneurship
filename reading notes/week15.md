# week 15 reading notes

Impact, Leadership & Futures, the close of the course (Module VI). Four readings on the difference the work makes, how you lead toward it, and where it grows, alongside the integrative Portfolio Overview Statement. The week returns to the impact and access thread that opened Module I (outcomes 5 and 1). Summaries drawn from the chapters.

## Rabideau, ch. 2, "Exploring Curiosity: Finding Opportunities to Make a Community Impact" (pp. 17–30)

The impact chapter. Rabideau frames curiosity as what drives artists to explore, push the boundaries of the form, and take on societal questions, and turns it toward community impact and toward the question "why?", the meaning and purpose under the work (his exercises are Find Your Why and Locate Your Genius). His examples make the point that art changes how people see: John Cage redefining the audience and challenging assumptions with 4'33", and Vincent Laforet using aerial photography to make the scale of climate change visible. The through-line is that a practice can be built around the difference it makes for a community.

key ideas: curiosity as directed toward community impact; the search for your why; art as changing how people see.

## Andrews, ch. 7, "Cultural Policy and the Arts Entrepreneur" (pp. 208–239)

The public frame. Andrews situates the individual artist inside the larger system through which societies fund and shape the arts. He traces cultural policy across countries (France as the deep case, from royal patronage through the French Revolution to the Ministry of Culture) and ends on the two themes that recur, access, or democratizing culture, and excellence, alongside the notion of cultural rights (written into the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights). From there he covers US cultural policy, government grants and arts agencies, taxes and 501(c)(3) status, and censorship. The point for this week is that the arts have a public value, and access, who gets to make and reach culture, has been the recurring question for centuries, which ties this close back to the power-and-access work of week 5.

key ideas: the arts' public value; cultural policy as how societies support and shape the arts; access as the recurring question; the tie back to Module I.

## McCurdy, ch. 8, "Arts Leadership" (chapter)

The leadership chapter. McCurdy's central claim is that leadership can be learned rather than only inherited, and that the emerging artist builds it from their own strengths (the SWOT analysis done earlier is the starting point). He notes that talent and creativity give artists instant credibility, that leadership is rarely taught in the arts curriculum, and that there is no single formula. The correction he presses is against "lead by example" as a complete theory: leading also means communicating your goals and vision clearly and enthusiastically, not just modeling them.

key ideas: leadership is learnable and built from your strengths; communicating a vision, not only leading by example.

## Kolb, ch. 11, "Expanding the business" (pp. 249–271)

The futures chapter. Kolb turns to how an established practice grows: additional distribution (consignment, where a store takes forty to sixty percent but provides the space and marketing; a cooperative store; a retail arrangement), scaling up, and adopting a new form such as a nonprofit if it fits the mission. His running caution is to weigh the trade-offs of each growth step and to get the terms in writing before signing on.

key ideas: growth options (new distribution, scale, a change of form); the trade-offs each involves.

## how they fit together

The four cover the week's outward turn. Rabideau and Andrews are impact: the difference you want to make and the why beneath it, set inside the public value the arts have and the access question that runs through cultural policy, which returns to Module I and outcomes 5 and 1. McCurdy is leadership, the learnable capacity to move a practice toward that impact by communicating a vision. Kolb is futures, the practical routes a practice grows along. The week's other work is integration: the Portfolio Overview Statement names the through-line across all the components so the portfolio reads as one document, and serves as the introduction to the final portfolio compiled and presented in finals week. The reflection asks for the societal impact the student wants their practice to have, and one concrete way to pursue it.
